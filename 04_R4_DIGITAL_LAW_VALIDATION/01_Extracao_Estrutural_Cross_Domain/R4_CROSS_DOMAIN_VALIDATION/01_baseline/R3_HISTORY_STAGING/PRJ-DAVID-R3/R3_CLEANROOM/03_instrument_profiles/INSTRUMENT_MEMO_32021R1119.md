# 1. Executive summary

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 1-2, 4-5]** This Regulation establishes the European Climate Law framework: climate neutrality by 2050, a 2030 net domestic reduction target of at least 55% against 1990, adaptation progress and a future process for a 2040 target.

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 3-11]** The framework joins binding goals with scientific advice, adaptation planning, recurring assessment of collective progress and policy consistency, public participation, sectoral roadmaps and review.

**[ANALYTICAL_INFERENCE]** The declared orientation is framework-setting and review-oriented climate governance.

# 2. Declared regulatory objective

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Article 1]** The declared objective is an irreversible and gradual reduction of regulated anthropogenic emissions and enhancement of removals, with binding climate neutrality in the Union by 2050 and progress toward the Paris adaptation goal.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 2 and 4]** The Regulation sets the binding 2050 neutrality objective and the binding 2030 target, while requiring future action toward a 2040 target.

# 3. Regulatory object

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 1-5]** The regulatory object is the Union climate objective system: emissions and removals regulated in Union law, climate targets, adaptation progress and the consistency of measures with those objectives.

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 3, 6-11]** The object also includes the scientific, planning, assessment and participation framework through which collective climate action is guided and reviewed.

# 4. Principal target classes

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 2, 4-7, 9-10]** The broad governed classes are Union institutions and Member States, whose measures, strategies, targets and progress contribute to collective climate neutrality and adaptation.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 5, 9-10 and Article 13 amendments]** Economic sectors, social partners, academia, business, citizens, civil society and local/regional actors appear as broad participants or subjects of engagement, not as relation-specific target classifications.

# 5. Principal regulatory authorities

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 3-11]** The Commission sets or proposes strategic elements, assesses Union and national measures, reports to Parliament and Council, facilitates participation and engages with sectors. Member States adopt measures and national adaptation strategies and participate in climate governance.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Article 3; Article 12 amendment]** The European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change provides independent scientific advice and reports. The EEA assists assessments, while the amended Energy Union governance system carries planning, reporting and dialogue functions.

# 6. Legal scope

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Article 1]** Material scope covers anthropogenic emissions by sources and removals by sinks of greenhouse gases listed in Part 2 of Annex V to Regulation 2018/1999 and regulated in Union law.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 1-5]** Territorial scope is Union-wide with Member State implementation. Temporal scope is climate neutrality by 2050, a 2030 target, a future 2040 target process, recurring five-year assessments and reviews tied to Paris Agreement global stocktakes.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 3-5, 9-10]** The act distinguishes binding objectives and duties from invited national advisory bodies, voluntary sectoral roadmaps and participatory dialogue.

# 7. Major obligation clusters

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 1-4]** Goal setting: the Regulation fixes 2050 neutrality and 2030 reduction objectives and mandates a future 2040 target proposal and indicative budget process.

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 3 and 5]** Scientific and adaptation governance: independent scientific advice, Union adaptation strategy, national adaptation strategies, vulnerability analysis and climate-risk guidance support the framework.

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 6-8]** Assessment and consistency control: the Commission assesses collective progress, national measures and Union measures against climate and adaptation objectives, using specified scientific and reporting inputs.

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 9-10 and 13]** Participation and sectoral planning: inclusive public engagement, science-based information, climate dialogues and voluntary sectoral roadmaps connect broad social and economic participation to the framework.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT — Articles 11-13]** Review and institutional integration: global-stocktake reports, possible legislative proposals, the Scientific Advisory Board and amendments to Energy Union planning and reporting integrate the framework into wider Union governance.

# 8. Main governance components

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 2-13]** The components are: binding goal setting; scientific advice; adaptation strategy and national plans; collective progress assessment; policy-consistency assessment; public participation; sectoral dialogue and roadmaps; Paris-cycle review; integration with Energy Union planning and reporting.

# 9. Structural logic

**[CROSS_PROVISION_RECONSTRUCTION — Articles 2-11]** binding 2050/2030 goals -> scientific and adaptation framework -> Union and national measures -> Commission assessment of progress and consistency -> public and sectoral engagement -> global-stocktake review and future target adjustment.

# 10. Declared regulatory orientation

**[ANALYTICAL_INFERENCE — Articles 1-11]** Framework-setting and review-oriented climate governance structure. The Regulation organizes the direction, evidence, assessment and feedback of climate policy across time rather than prescribing one sector-specific compliance market.

# 12. Analytical uncertainties

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT / ANALYTICAL_UNCERTAINTY — Article 4(3)-(6)]** The 2040 target is not fixed by this original act; it is to be proposed through a later legislative process informed by stocktakes and assessment.

**[EXPLICIT_TEXT / ANALYTICAL_UNCERTAINTY — Articles 9-10]** Public participation and sectoral roadmaps are important governance components, but the roadmaps are expressly voluntary and this phase does not infer enforceable duties from participation language.
