# 1. RESEARCH CONTEXT

The broader research project examines **regulatory intermediation** through relational structures involving:

* regulators (`R`);
* regulatory intermediaries (`I`);
* regulatory targets (`T`);
* R–I relationships;
* I–T relationships;
* direct R–T relationships;
* intermediary mechanisms;
* legal evidence supporting every analytical decision.

R1–R3 developed and progressively refined a coding and evidence architecture using EU green regulation.

R4 was proposed as a **cross-domain validation exercise**.

The objective is to determine whether the analytical framework constructed using green regulation can travel to digital regulation.

The comparison must help answer:

1. Which variables are genuinely necessary?
2. Which variables generate excessive coding burden?
3. Which variables travel successfully across regulatory domains?
4. Which tasks can AI perform reliably?
5. Which tasks require human qualitative interpretation?
6. When is escalation from a lower-cost to a more capable model justified?
7. How does regulatory intermediation differ between green and digital governance?
8. Where does the existing R3 framework fail, become ambiguous, or lose information?

R4 therefore evaluates both:

**the regulatory intermediation framework**

and

**the methodological architecture through which humans and AI construct the dataset.**

# 5. MODEL ARCHITECTURE

## 5.1 GPT-5.6 Luna High

Use **GPT-5.6 Luna High** as the default model for tasks characterized by:

* low interpretive complexity;
* high repetition;
* structured operations;
* explicit legal text;
* deterministic transformations;
* routine extraction;
* normalization;
* validation;
* quality control.

Its use is motivated primarily by **cost-effectiveness**.

Typical Luna High tasks include:

* file inventory;
* metadata extraction;
* CELEX validation;
* official-title extraction;
* date extraction;
* article indexing;
* provision segmentation;
* locating explicit actor names;
* locating explicit institutional references;
* keyword-supported candidate retrieval;
* extracting explicit legal verbs;
* normalization;
* deduplication;
* schema checks;
* table formatting;
* consistency validation;
* simple reconciliation;
* missing-field detection;
* mechanical cross-checking;
* file naming;
* deterministic transformations.

Luna High may generate **candidate analytical records**, but it must not silently convert difficult legal relationships into final conceptual decisions.

# 6. GPT-5.6 TERRA HIGH

Use **GPT-5.6 Terra High** for tasks involving substantive complexity or theoretical inference.

Terra High is mandatory or strongly preferred for:

* complex legal reading;
* interpretation of long or cross-referenced provisions;
* identification of regulatory intermediaries;
* distinguishing R, I and T;
* identification of regulatory mechanisms;
* mechanism classification;
* ambiguous delegation structures;
* hybrid public–private arrangements;
* recursive or nested intermediation;
* assurance architectures;
* auditing;
* certification;
* conformity assessment;
* monitoring architectures;
* supervisory structures;
* institutional layering;
* ambiguous actor roles;
* cases in which an actor may simultaneously occupy more than one regulatory role;
* difficult boundary cases;
* adjudication of classifications;
* conceptual inconsistencies;
* conflicts between textual evidence and existing categories.

Most importantly:

> **Substantive identification and classification of regulatory intermediation mechanisms must use Terra High whenever inference beyond explicit textual extraction is required.**

# 7. MODEL ESCALATION RULE

The research workflow follows:

**Luna High → Terra High → Human Adjudication**

when complexity increases.

Escalate from Luna High to Terra High when any of the following occurs:

* more than one plausible R–I–T configuration exists;
* the intermediary status of an actor is uncertain;
* mechanism classification is ambiguous;
* multiple mechanisms overlap;
* the relevant legal rule is distributed across provisions;
* recitals and operative provisions appear to pull in different directions;
* institutional function cannot be inferred from the actor label alone;
* the same actor appears to be regulator and intermediary;
* the same actor appears to be intermediary and target;
* legal wording permits multiple analytically defensible classifications;
* Luna returns inconsistent classifications across similar provisions;
* the R3 taxonomy does not clearly accommodate the digital arrangement;
* substantive governance interpretation becomes necessary.

Escalation is not evidence that Luna failed.

It indicates that the task crossed the pre-defined complexity threshold.

# 10. EXTRACTION IS NOT INTERPRETATION

A fundamental methodological rule is:

> **Extraction and interpretation are different research operations.**

Examples of extraction:

* locating “independent auditor”;
* extracting Article 37;
* finding “shall monitor”;
* identifying a reporting duty;
* locating a certification provision.

These operations may often be delegated to Luna High.

Examples of interpretation:

* deciding whether an auditor is a regulatory intermediary;
* deciding who is R and who is T;
* determining whether monitoring constitutes the primary mechanism;
* distinguishing verification from evaluation;
* deciding whether certification mediates or merely records compliance;
* identifying a nested intermediation chain.

These operations may require Terra High and/or human adjudication.

Do not treat successful extraction as successful theoretical classification.

---

# 11. MECHANISM IDENTIFICATION PROTOCOL

Mechanism identification is a **high-inference operation**.

It must receive special methodological treatment.

Do not classify a mechanism merely because a provision contains words such as:

* monitor;
* report;
* audit;
* certify;
* assess;
* inspect;
* verify;
* advise;
* notify;
* accredit;
* supervise;
* transmit;
* evaluate.

These terms are **search signals**, not mechanism decisions.

For every candidate mechanism, determine:

1. **Who acts?**
2. **Who authorizes or structures the action?**
3. **Who or what is affected?**
4. **What regulatory relationship is being mediated?**
5. **What information, obligation, judgment, certification, assurance, or regulatory signal passes through the intermediary?**
6. **What is the regulatory function of the action?**
7. **What legal provision establishes the function?**
8. **Is the actor actually between regulator and target?**
9. **Does the R3 mechanism taxonomy adequately capture the action?**
10. **Is the mechanism explicit or analytically inferred?**

# 13. CONFIDENCE RULE

Use at minimum:

* `HIGH`
* `MEDIUM`
* `LOW`

### HIGH

Use when:

* legal evidence is explicit;
* actor role is clear;
* relational structure is clear;
* mechanism fits the existing taxonomy with little inference.

### MEDIUM

Use when:

* legal evidence is clear;
* but the analytical classification requires interpretation.

### LOW

Use when:

* more than one plausible interpretation remains;
* intermediary status is unclear;
* mechanism boundaries are unstable;
* evidence is indirect;
* the R3 taxonomy fits poorly.

Low-confidence cases must not disappear through forced classification.

They must remain visible.

---

# 14. HUMAN REVIEW TRIGGERS

Human review is mandatory when:

* confidence is LOW;
* Terra High reports multiple plausible classifications;
* R–I–T roles overlap;
* mechanism categories overlap materially;
* a new mechanism appears necessary;
* a new variable appears necessary;
* the R3 codebook seems inadequate;
* legal provisions conflict;
* evidence is mainly inferential;
* the AI proposes changing a theoretical rule;
* different model runs generate materially different outputs;
* model output conflicts with prior adjudicated decisions.
