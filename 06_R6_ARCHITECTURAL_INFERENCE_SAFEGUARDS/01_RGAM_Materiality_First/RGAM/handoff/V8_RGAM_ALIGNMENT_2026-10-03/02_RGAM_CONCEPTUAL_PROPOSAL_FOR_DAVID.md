# A possible morphological specification for relational leximetrics

## A. Starting from V8

V8 provides the starting point, rather than a gap to be displaced. It defines governance architecture as the legally constituted configuration through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed among actors and organized through relationships, mechanisms and instruments. Its relational leximetrics approach makes the relationship a pivotal bridge between legal text and higher-order inference; it distinguishes units of coding, analysis and inference; and it requires configurational rather than merely additive aggregation, vertical traceability and a stopping rule.

V8 also sharply distinguishes a governance architecture from a regulatory regime. Regimes are broader institutional systems and may be nested, embedded, layered, overlapping, cross-cutting, coupled, or latent and functionally reconstructed. The new distinction between architecture within regimes and architecture among regimes is especially important: it makes regime boundaries an empirical and theoretical question rather than a consequence of statutory location.

The following is therefore offered as a possible *morphological specification* of this framework, not as a rival account of architecture.

## B. Proposed morphological specification

The proposal begins from a simple observation: a configuration may have properties that are not properties of any isolated component. An actor's legal power is an intrinsic property; supervision, reporting or verification is a relational property; being a necessary stage in a specified legal sequence is a positional property; and a distribution of authority across a configuration is a whole-level property. Keeping these bearers distinct can help prevent the atomistic inference from one provision to an architecture, and the ecological inference from a whole to each constituent.

It also preserves the legal meaning of connections. A governance relationship is not an unqualified graph edge. Where evidence warrants it, the relationship should retain its parties, type, direction, modality, conditions, temporal validity, legal provenance and relevant authority, discretion or accountability attributes. A graph remains a useful analytic view, but it is not the legal arrangement itself.

This proposal initially reconstructs the **formal legally constituted layer of potentially broader governance architectures**. It does not define governance in general. In particular, it does not deny V8's inclusion of non-state capacity, standards, contracts, market arrangements or operational intermediation. It simply keeps legal constitution, material/institutional instantiation and observed operation as different claims.

## C. Analytical structure

```
W: declared documentary universe (version, time, domain and scope)
        │ evidence-backed inference
E → P → R → S → A
│   │   │   │   └─ reconstructed architecture / property profile
│   │   │   └──── candidate, possibly overlapping subconfiguration
│   │   └──────── qualified governance relationships
│   └──────────── positions and distinguishable institutional devices
└──────────────── legal evidence and institutional statements

M: transversal evidentiary/material anchoring     X: adjacent implementation and operation
```

The diagram is not a mandatory linear hierarchy. It expresses a discipline of claims. **W is not A**: a statute, or even a multi-instrument corpus, fixes what has been examined; it does not itself establish a single integrated architecture. Normative assignments are also kept separate from R. An obligation, permission or competence assigned to a position should become a governance relationship only where a governance-relevant counterparty is evidenced—not because a binary graph would be convenient. A Hohfeldian correlative alone does not settle that analytical relevance.

The proposed I1–I4 criteria are not a test of whether an architecture exists in a metaphysical sense. They are provisional warrant conditions for advancing a reconstruction claim.

| Criterion | Question for the proposed claim |
|---|---|
| **I1. Problem and scale** | What governance problem, domain, time and analytical scale does the candidate whole organize? |
| **I2. Qualified coupling** | What legally or institutionally supported connection links the relevant subconfigurations? |
| **I3. Configurational consequence** | What property arises from their arrangement; who or what bears it; and what inference rule supports it? |
| **I4. Boundary and alternatives** | What rival grouping, unresolved dependency, limitation or uncertainty could change the conclusion? |

A counterfactual removal or boundary-change exercise can sometimes serve as a sensitivity check. It is not proposed as a universal test: architectures may have redundant, substitutable or multiple pathways.

## D. Architectural boundaries and broader regimes

V8's regime-architecture discussion suggests a careful future extension. A provisional notation **G** could denote regulatory regimes. One A might participate in more than one G, and relations among G could then be investigated at a different analytical scale. This would not mean that a regime is a graph cluster, that all observations in one documentary corpus belong to one regime, or that a regime-level ontology has already been validated. It is a research direction requiring a boundary criterion and, ideally, guidance on the threshold for latent functional regimes.

## E. Limited legal illustration: GDPR cooperation and consistency

*Exploratory, machine-assisted legal illustration; not independently validated.*

The existing GDPR Pilot 01 records a type-level candidate subconfiguration concerning cross-border cooperation and consistency. Article 56(1) assigns the lead supervisory authority competence for cross-border processing under the Article 60 procedure. Article 60 provides the cooperation and objection sequence among the lead and concerned authorities. Where the lead does not follow a relevant and reasoned objection, Article 60(4) directs the matter to the consistency mechanism; Articles 63 and 65 provide for a binding Board decision, and Article 65(6) requires the lead authority to adopt its final decision on that basis. Articles 64 and 66 add opinion, standstill and urgency paths.

The legally specified sequence supports a candidate claim about distributed first-instance authority and concentrated dispute-stage authority. It does not rest on graph reachability. It also remains bounded: supervisory authorities are constituted through Member State law outside the pilot corpus, the European Data Protection Supervisor is external to it, and reviewability of Board decisions is outside its scope. The pilot therefore treats the result as a provisional, type-level reconstruction, not a validated characterization of the GDPR in practice. As a contrast, SC-15 contains 35 heterogeneous normative assignments without an identifiable counterpart; these are not automatically converted into 35 actor-to-actor relationships.
