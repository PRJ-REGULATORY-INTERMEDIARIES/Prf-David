# From Architectural Configuration to Regulatory Governance Architecture: Towards a Morphological and Materially Anchored Ontology of Law

**Igor Caires Machado**

*Working draft v0.4 — 3 October 2026. Conceptual-methodological article. Not peer reviewed; no empirical validation is claimed. Authorship arrangements are not agreed and none is presumed. The article takes as its starting point an unpublished draft by David Levi-Faur (V6, 2 October 2026), cited here pending the author's permission. Items marked `[CITATION CHECK]` rest on scholarship whose primary text has not yet been verified by the author; a register of verification status follows the reference list.*

## Abstract

Law does not only prescribe, prohibit and permit conduct; it also constitutes the arrangements through which conduct is governed. Levi-Faur's recent relational-leximetrics framework gives this proposition a precise form: a governance architecture is the legally constituted configuration through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed among actors and organized through relationships, mechanisms and instruments, and it is an emergent property of that configuration. Such a definition invites a further question that the framework deliberately leaves open: what kind of entity is each of its constructs once an architecture is to be reconstructed empirically, which properties belong to a part and which to the whole, and what entitles a researcher to say that an architecture has been reconstructed rather than merely designed? We ask how a materially anchored and configurational ontology can extend relational leximetrics so that regulatory governance architecture can be reconstructed in an empirically verifiable way while properties of constituent parts are distinguished from those of the architectural whole. We draw on the configurational tradition of architectural theory (Hillier and Hanson) for the analytic structure of part and whole, on critical realism (Bhaskar) for the ontology and epistemology of reconstruction, and on a substantive concept of materiality, which distinguishes documentary inscription, legal constitution, material instantiation and observed operation, to connect constructs to identifiable referents. We propose an ontology of evidence, positions, qualified relations, subconfigurations and a bounded whole, with evidentiary anchoring running across all strata, and show that the twelve constructs of the framework take different ontological roles, only some of which are nodes. We draw implications for relational leximetrics and state the limits of the proposal. The argument is conceptual; empirical validation is the next task.

**Keywords:** regulatory governance; governance architecture; relational leximetrics; configurational theory; critical realism; materiality; ontology of law

## 1. Introduction: when is governance an architecture?

What kind of governance does a law build? Levi-Faur (2026, §1) puts the question to comparative legal research, which has developed sophisticated ways of comparing what laws require, how much they cover and how stringent they are, but has far fewer tools for comparing how laws organize the work of governing. The intuition behind the question is familiar from regulatory scholarship. Two statutes may impose similar substantive requirements while one relies on direct public inspection, another on firms' own risk-management systems, and a third on market access conditional on certification by accredited private bodies. The difference between them is not located in any one rule, actor or instrument; it lies in how these are arranged.

V6, the draft on which this article builds, gives this intuition a definition and a method. A governance architecture is "the legally constituted configuration through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed among actors and organized through relationships, mechanisms and instruments" (Levi-Faur 2026, §5.2). It is "an emergent property of a configuration" that "cannot normally be inferred from the presence of a single instrument, relationship or actor" (§5.2); it is best understood multidimensionally rather than as a set of mutually exclusive types (§5.2); and "counts identify composition; configurations identify organization" (§6.6). The method, relational leximetrics, takes a legally constituted relationship as the pivotal empirical object and moves from legal text through institutional statements, relationships and configurations to architectures and regimes under explicit rules of inference, a stopping rule and a catalogue of cross-level fallacies (§§6.2–6.10). The twelve constructs of the framework are arranged in three levels: objects, actors, functions, mechanisms, instruments and relationships; authority, discretion and accountability as relational attributes; and strategies, architectures and governance logics as higher-order constructions (§§3–5).

This article takes that construct as given. Its aim is not to discover that governance has an architecture, nor to replace the framework, but to specify it. Specification becomes necessary the moment one asks what it would take to *reconstruct* an architecture from legal sources and to defend the result. Three questions arise.

1. **Ontological roles.** What kind of thing is each of the twelve constructs when an architecture is reconstructed? An actor, a function and a mechanism are plainly not the same kind of entity, yet a network drawing treats them alike if each becomes a vertex.
2. **Bearers of properties.** Which properties belong to a constituent part, which to a relationship, which to a local arrangement and which to the architecture as a whole? V6 warns against the atomistic fallacy (lifting a property from a part to the whole) and the ecological fallacy (pushing a whole's property down to each part); the warnings presuppose a way of saying where a property resides.
3. **Warrant of reconstruction.** What entitles a researcher to describe an architecture as empirically reconstructed rather than designed, projected or merely drawn? V6 separates formal legal, implementation and operational architecture, and observes that legal coding reconstructs primarily the first. How that separation is to be maintained when a source exists, an agency is established on paper, or a register is mandated but unbuilt, calls for a theory of what supports a legally constituted relation.

There is also a risk in the word itself. "Architecture" is often used as an umbrella label for any non-trivial arrangement of governing actors, and used so it explains little. The discipline that gave the term to the social sciences has worked out, over a long tradition, what it takes for an arrangement to be an *architecture*: a bounded whole, parts that are defined by their relations to other parts, properties that appear only at the level of arrangement, and a persistent distinction between the plan, the thing built and the thing used. Our contention is that this disciplinary concept, read alongside a realist account of what reconstruction can and cannot establish, supplies what the three questions require, without displacing the substantive scholarship of regulation that V6 draws on.

The research question is therefore:

> *How can a materially anchored and configurational ontology extend Levi-Faur's relational leximetrics, enabling empirically verifiable reconstruction of regulatory governance architecture while distinguishing properties of constituent parts from those of the architectural whole?*

The argument proceeds as follows. Section 2 asks what architecture means as a disciplinary concept. Section 3 presents the configurational theory of Hillier and Hanson and justifies its selection against alternatives. Section 4 turns to critical realism and materiality and to the epistemological problem of reconstruction. Section 5 examines the transposition from architectural morphology to regulatory governance, its conditions and its limits. Section 6 proposes the ontology. Section 7 draws implications for relational leximetrics. Section 8 states the contribution, the limitations and the programme of empirical validation.

Three clarifications of scope. The article is conceptual and methodological: it reports no reconstruction of an actual legal regime and no test of reliability. Morphology is its principal analytical object; materiality is its empirical anchor; sociomateriality, the study of how arrangements are enacted in practice, is treated as a complementary perspective and not as the object of study. And the article does not claim priority over existing work on regulatory intermediation, indirect governance or the "architecture" of governance regimes; where such literatures bear on the argument they are named and their verification status is recorded.

## 2. Architecture as a disciplinary concept

### 2.1 Three senses of architecture

In the discipline of architecture the word is used in at least three senses that are routinely distinguished and routinely run together. In the first, architecture is an *artefact*: the building, its fabric and its form. In the second, it is a *design*: an intention to organize, expressed in drawings, specifications and codes before and apart from any construction. In the third, it is a *configuration*: the way spaces and the relations of access and separation among them are arranged, so that the building supports some patterns of encounter, movement and avoidance and impedes others. The three are connected, since a design is realized in an artefact whose configuration is then inhabited, but they are not the same and they come apart in practice. Many buildings differ from their drawings; many are used in ways their designers did not foresee.

The configurational sense is the one at issue for governance. Hillier's work, discussed in the next section, argues that what is distinctive in architecture as a field of knowledge is precisely the configuration of spatial relations and its social consequences, not the object as such or the designer's intention (Hillier 1996; `[CITATION CHECK]` against the primary text). Levi-Faur's definition is configurational in this sense: it places architecture in how governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are *distributed and organized*, and not in the mere presence of actors or instruments.

The distinction between design, built form and use has a direct counterpart in V6, which separates formal legal architecture, implementation architecture and operational architecture (Levi-Faur 2026, §6.13). We return to the parallel in Section 4: it explains why a legal text, like a drawing, is evidence of a plan and is not by itself evidence that anything has been built or used.

### 2.2 From label to claim

Used as a label, "architecture" is cheap. Used as a *claim*, it carries conditions, and it is useful to state them, because they are what the later sections will specify. An arrangement of governing actors is an architecture, in the strong sense relevant here, only if four conditions hold.

- **Boundedness.** The arrangement is delimited: there is a stated corpus, jurisdiction, domain and period, and a stated rule for what falls inside. Without a boundary there is no whole, and any property of "the architecture" is undefined. V6 recognizes this when it insists that one statute is not automatically one architecture and that moving from a law to a regime requires an explicit boundary decision (§§5.2.3, 6.7).
- **Relational organization.** The parts are defined by qualified relations among them, not by an inventory. An agency, an auditor and a firm are the same three parts in many different arrangements.
- **Emergent properties.** The arrangement has properties that are not properties of any single part or relation and that cannot be obtained by listing them. Centralization, relational depth, the degree to which a function is routed through third parties and the closure of accountability are of this kind.
- **Recoverability.** The arrangement can be recovered from identifiable sources by a procedure that can be repeated and challenged.

These conditions are not drawn from any single source; they are the conditions under which the claim that governance *has an architecture* can be tested rather than asserted. They are also the conditions that the label usage fails to meet: when "architecture" is applied to a loose cluster of institutions with no boundary, no stated relations and no way of showing the claim wrong, the word adds nothing to "arrangement".

### 2.3 Neighbouring concepts

Architecture has to be distinguished from several concepts that regulatory scholars use close by. *Structure* is more general and does not by itself imply a bounded whole. *System* implies functional interdependence that may or may not be configurational. *Design* names intention, which in law may be recorded in preparatory materials but is not the same as the arrangement the law constitutes. *Regime* names a wider and more variably bounded set of rules and institutions; V6 reserves it for the sixth and last step of its inference ladder, where the boundary has to be explicitly decided. *Organization* is typically an actor and appears inside architectures as a part. Treating architecture as the bounded configuration of legally constituted positions and relations keeps it analytically distinct from each of these while remaining continuous with V6.

The term has also travelled into systems engineering, enterprise architecture and the study of the "architecture" of international governance, each with its own usage. We do not rely on those usages here. Whether any of them already supplies a configurational method for regulatory governance is a question for the literature review that a journal submission will require and which this draft has not completed (Section 8).

### 2.4 What the disciplinary concept supplies

Four things, in short: an object (the configuration), a requirement (a bounded whole), a logic (parts defined by their relations to other parts, and properties emerging at the level of arrangement) and a discipline (keep apart plan, built form and use). The next section turns to the theory that has developed the second and third of these most fully.

## 3. Hillier and Hanson: configurational theory and its comparative justification

### 3.1 Configuration as an analytic concept

Hillier and Hanson's *The Social Logic of Space* (1984) develops what became known as space syntax, a theory and a method for describing the spatial organization of buildings and settlements and for relating it to social patterns. Four ideas are of interest here, stated in our own words and subject to verification against the primary texts (`[CITATION CHECK]` for each).

First, space is not a neutral container. The way spaces are laid out is itself a social and cultural product that carries and shapes patterns of co-presence, encounter and control. Second, the unit of analysis is the *configuration*: the relations among spaces considered with respect to all the others, not the spaces taken one at a time. A given space is "deep" or "shallow", "integrated" or "segregated", in virtue of its position in the whole. Third, the method describes arrangements through graphs of access relations among spaces and derives measures of depth and integration from them, so that two arrangements built from the same rooms can be shown to differ. Fourth, *Ideas Are in Things* (Hillier, Hanson and Graham 1987) applies the approach to detect recurrent configurational patterns, "genotypes", in sets of houses, using observable configuration as evidence about underlying cultural principles.

The theory therefore contributes a particular way of treating the relation between a part and a whole: the properties of a part that matter are *positional*, defined by its relations to the rest, and the properties of the whole are emergent from the pattern of those relations.

### 3.2 Why configuration matters for the V6 construct

V6's definition of architecture asserts emergence; it also states the fallacies that follow from neglecting it. What the construct does not itself say is how emergent and positional properties are to be *identified* and *attributed*. Consider the example V6 uses to introduce the point: a statute requiring that assessments be verified by an accredited auditor who notifies a supervisory authority, which may suspend a provider's authorization (Levi-Faur 2026, §6.15). Each of the four provisions can be coded as a separate observation. That an auditor is "consequential to the operation of the regulatory process" is not, however, a property of the auditor's provision; it is a property of the auditor's position in the whole, and depends on what else the law provides. A configurational vocabulary makes this explicit: the same part-level statement ("the auditor verifies the provider") can occur in arrangements in which the auditor is a necessary stage and in arrangements in which it is not, and the architectural claim differs accordingly. We return to this in Section 6.

### 3.3 Comparative justification

A framework for specifying V6 should be chosen against explicit criteria, and the criteria should come from V6's own problem and not from the framework. Nine criteria are suggested by the construct and its method.

| | Criterion | Source in V6 |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Configurational primacy: properties arise from the arrangement of relations | architecture as emergent (§5.2) |
| C2 | Part/whole separation: a place to say which properties belong to which scale | atomistic and ecological fallacies (§6.8) |
| C3 | Qualified and typed relations: edges are not observationally equivalent | relations as pivotal and typed (§6.3) |
| C4 | Traceability to inscription | vertical traceability (§6.9) |
| C5 | Stratification: formal, implemented, operational; existence is not observability | §6.13; functional and implementation fallacies |
| C6 | Plural and overlapping architectures | §§5.2, 5.2.3 |
| C7 | Explicit boundary and scale | container fallacy; regime boundary (§§6.7, 6.8) |
| C8 | Non-displacement: it must not turn the project into implementation studies, nor require every construct to be coded at once | V6 as conceptual map, not codebook |
| C9 | Honest transfer: if borrowed, state what transfers and what does not | avoidance of analogy by label |

Against these criteria we compared the following candidates. The ratings are the author's judgment, offered for scrutiny and not as findings.

*An inventory or typology* (the constructs and the named architectures taken as categories, without further structure) satisfies traceability and non-displacement but cannot express C1, C2 or C7: it lists what may be present and says nothing about arrangement. *Network analysis* of legal relations is attractive because it is computational and accommodates plural patterns, and V6 itself envisages its use; but it does not by itself provide traceability to inscription or a stratified ontology, and V6 warns that network aggregation "risks abstracting from the substantive legal meaning" of relationships (§6.6). *Institutional grammar* (Crawford and Ostrom 1995; Frantz and Siddiki 2021) decomposes institutional statements into actors, actions, deontic operators, conditions and consequences; it is strong at the statement level, which V6 places at the second step of its ladder, and it feeds the reconstruction, but configuration emerges only above that level. *Legal and normative ontologies and knowledge graphs* are strong on typed relations, provenance and versioned legal text and offer valuable modelling discipline, but they model norms and do not define what an architecture is or how whole-level properties are attributed. *Systems-architecture description frameworks* offer explicit boundary and multiple-view discipline, but presuppose an engineered system with stakeholders and concerns, and are not examined further here. *Critical realism* provides a stratified ontology and an account of emergence but is a philosophy of science, not a method for reading legal text. *Sociomateriality* offers a powerful account of enacted practice but, as a primary frame, would pull the study toward implementation (C8). *Policy-instrument and regulatory theories* (Lascoumes and Le Galès 2007; Abbott, Levi-Faur and Snidal 2017; and others on which V6 draws) are the substantive source of the constructs rather than competitors for the structural task.

No candidate meets all nine criteria. The result is a division of labour. V6 supplies the substance. Critical realism supplies the ontology and epistemology of reconstruction (Section 4). The configurational tradition supplies the part/whole structure. Institutional grammar feeds the statement level. Legal-ontology practice supplies modelling hygiene. Sociomateriality is a complementary lens on enactment. Configurational theory is chosen for the *structural* task because it is the candidate that most directly addresses C1, C2 and, with adaptation, C6 and C7, and because it does so as a theory of how arrangement produces properties and not as a computational technique alone.

### 3.4 What configurational theory adds beyond an inventory of V6's categories

The claim of incremental value must be stated so that it can fail. We suggest five contributions, none of which is a discovery about governance.

1. **A treatment of emergence.** V6 asserts that architecture emerges from configuration; the configurational tradition treats the properties of a position as determined by its relations to all others. This yields a class of *positional* properties (being a necessary stage; depth from the rule-making position) that cannot be read from a part's own text.
2. **A contrast design.** If arrangements with the same elements and different relations differ in their properties, then holding the inventory constant and varying the relations is a way of testing whether an architectural claim depends on configuration. V6 makes the point qualitatively (two laws may use similar devices and construct different architectures); the design makes it testable in principle.
3. **Local arrangement and global structure.** The distinction maps onto subconfigurations and the whole and disciplines the generalization of local findings, which is what V6's container and ecological fallacies guard against.
4. **Boundary and threshold as analytic objects.** The tradition treats the points at which access passes from one space to another as significant. Their counterpart in governance is the *interface*, for example a register or an accreditation step through which governing work passes between public and private positions. V6 has dimensions that touch it (public-private allocation, self-regulatory autonomy) but no explicit class.
5. **An epistemological parallel.** *Ideas Are in Things* is concerned with inferring social principles from observable configuration. This parallels, without being identical to, V6's route from legal text to configurations and, with care, to governance logics. It is a parallel in the structure of the inference and not a claim that space syntax and relational leximetrics are the same technique.

The claim of incremental value *fails* if the contrast design cannot be instantiated in real legal text, or if V6's ten architectural dimensions already capture contributions 1 to 4 without remainder. Both are empirical questions for the validation programme in Section 8.

### 3.5 Limits of the configurational tradition for law

The tradition does not supply a theory of regulation or of legal meaning. Its graphs are justified by movement and co-presence; legal relations are normatively constituted, directed, of many types, conditional, and subject to change and expiry. Its measures presuppose a well-defined adjacency and a metric of depth; legal relations have neither by default. We therefore propose to transfer the *analytic structure* and not the *measures*. This limit is developed in Section 5.

## 4. Bhaskar, materiality and the epistemological problem of reconstruction

### 4.1 The problem

Reconstructing a governance architecture from legal sources is an inferential task. The researcher reads inscriptions and infers statements, relations, configurations and finally an architecture. Two opposite errors threaten it. The first treats what is directly countable in the text as all there is: an architecture is the list of actors and provisions found. The second treats the architecture as merely the analyst's construction, with no claim to describe anything that exists independently of the analysis. V6's fallacies point at both, since the atomistic, frequency and container fallacies are errors of the first kind and the functional and implementation fallacies are errors of treating a formal assignment as a performed function. What is needed is an account of what an architecture is, in what sense it exists, and what a reconstruction may legitimately claim about it.

### 4.2 Critical realism as ontological and epistemological frame

Bhaskar's transcendental or critical realism (1975, 1979) offers such an account, in outline as follows `[CITATION CHECK]`. Reality is stratified: there are structures and powers (the real), the events that occur when they are exercised (the actual), and the experiences and observations through which events and structures are known (the empirical). What exists is not reduced to what is observed; to do so is the "epistemic fallacy", the collapse of questions about being into questions about knowing. Knowledge is nevertheless fallible and socially produced: the objects of inquiry are independent of it (the intransitive dimension), while theories and descriptions are its products (the transitive dimension). And wholes can have emergent properties that their parts lack. For the social world, Bhaskar argues that social structures depend on human activity and are yet not reducible to it; Archer's analytic dualism of structure and agency extends the argument (Archer 1995; `[CITATION CHECK]`, bibliographic record also unchecked).

This frame fits the problem. It licenses speaking of an architecture as something that may exist whether or not it has been recorded or observed, while requiring that claims about it be warranted by evidence and be revisable. It supplies a philosophical basis for the part/whole distinction (emergence). And it offers a loose correspondence with V6's tripartite distinction: formal architecture is a social structure constituted through authoritative inscriptions; operational architecture is a matter of events and practices; and what a reconstruction records is the empirical. The correspondence must not be pressed: V6's three architectures are not the three realist strata, and nothing here equates them.

One terminological hazard must be flagged. Bhaskar's "generative mechanism" is not V6's "mechanism", which denotes a means of governing such as command, disclosure or accreditation. In what follows "mechanism" has V6's meaning, and the realist notion, where needed, is written out in full.

### 4.3 Materiality as a substantive concern

If critical realism says an architecture can exist independently of its observation, why should a reconstruction require *material* referents? The answer lies in what kind of thing a legally constituted architecture is. It is not a physical object, and it is not an idea in the analyst's mind. It is a set of institutional arrangements that exist because they have been *inscribed* in authoritative texts, *constituted* with legal effect, *established* as offices, registers and procedures, and, in some cases, *enacted* in practice. Each of these is a different relation between the arrangement and the things that carry it. Materiality, as used here, is the theoretical concern with how a legally constituted architecture finds expression and support in identifiable inscriptions and institutional devices.

Materiality is not a synonym for a building, for physical adjacency, or for the existence of a PDF. Digital inscriptions and artefacts have material infrastructures too, and an enacted text, an official register and a platform are all material in the relevant sense. Nor is materiality synonymous with implementation. An architecture can have material expression in its constituting texts while its implementation is entirely unknown.

The architectural analogy of Section 2.1 is helpful here. A set of drawings is a material inscription of a design; a building is a material instantiation; use is a practice. A drawing may be authentic and complete without a building ever being constructed, and a building may be constructed and not used as intended. The relations between them are not inferences from one to another but separate matters of evidence.

We therefore distinguish four things that are often merged.

- **Documentary inscription.** An authentic, versioned document or trace exists, even if it is a draft, a repealed text or a non-binding guidance.
- **Legal constitution.** The inscribed norm has legal effect for the stated version and period: it is enacted, in force and applicable. A norm or arrangement can be legally constituted by means other than an enacted text (by custom or constitutional convention, for example), but such cases lie outside the scope of reconstruction from enacted sources and are flagged as such.
- **Material instantiation.** The position or device that the norm constitutes exists as an established, functioning entity: the office is filled, the register is accessible, the platform runs. This calls for evidence beyond the legal prescription.
- **Observed operation.** Practice is observed: use, compliance, appropriation. This is the object of the adjacent layer of enactment.

Corresponding to them are five *claim statuses*: conceptually projected (a design or ideal type with no enacted inscription), documented, legally constituted, materially instantiated and operationally observed. They are statuses of individual claims and not a life cycle. A practice may precede a formal basis; a norm may lapse while a practice persists; each status needs its own evidence and none can be inferred from another. A draft bill is documented but not legally constituted. A statute mandating a reporting platform that has not been built establishes the platform as a legally constituted device that is not instantiated. An accessible PDF of a statute shows documentation, and, with evidence of enactment and force, legal constitution, but shows nothing about whether the offices it creates are filled.

Materiality also bears on *relations*, not only on positions and devices. A legally constituted relation (say, that verifiers must record their results in a national register) *depends* on the entities through which it exists or is exercised. A relation has no body of its own, so instantiation is attributed to the positions and devices on which it depends, and the relation carries a record of its support dependencies and of any gaps. If practice occurs without the device, the evidence of practice stands and the gap is reported; the dependency is an expectation and not a gate. This is the sense in which materiality is more than provenance: provenance says where a datum came from, whereas materiality concerns what carries the constituted relation so that it can exist as law, be exercised and be shown.

### 4.4 Existence and observability: two theses

The apparent tension between realism and a materiality requirement dissolves once two theses are separated. The *ontological* thesis is weak: legal-institutional architecture is not free-floating; it is constituted by socially real structures that depend on inscriptions, offices, registers, practices and infrastructures, and "material" does not mean "physical". The *epistemic* thesis is stricter: a claim that an architecture has been *empirically reconstructed* must name the material referents and evidence through which it can be investigated, at the status claimed. The second thesis governs the warrant of the claim and not the existence of the thing. An operational arrangement may exist undocumented; the epistemic thesis says only that a reconstruction may not assert it as reconstructed. Correspondingly, without a defensible material referent we have an idea, an ideal type or a design of an architecture, and it must not be presented unqualified as one that has been empirically reconstructed.

*Evidentiary anchoring* is the methodological counterpart of materiality: the records, attached to claims, by which the dependencies and statuses just described are supported. The two are not synonyms. Materiality is a theoretical concept about how architectures are carried; evidentiary anchoring is the research practice that makes claims about that carrying checkable.

### 4.5 Sociomateriality as complementary lens

The sociomateriality literature, associated with Orlikowski (2007) and Orlikowski and Scott (2008), is generally read as a relational ontology in which the social and the material are constitutively entangled and entities lack inherent properties prior to their enactment (`[CITATION CHECK]` for the characterization). Leonardi (2012) discusses what the terms materiality and sociomateriality mean and how they differ, and argues for an approach that keeps the social and the material analytically distinct, which is closer to a stratified realism (`[CITATION CHECK]`). The two positions are not a consensus and should not be cited as one.

Our use is deliberately narrower than the literature's theoretical ambitions. Sociomateriality is applied to the layer of enactment: how configurations, once constituted and instantiated, are used, appropriated and transformed. It is not applied to the constitutive core of the ontology, and it is not reduced to implementation as a concept; the narrowing is a scope choice. This leaves an acknowledged limitation. A critical-realist frame and a constitutive-entanglement ontology are not jointly consistent as metatheories, so the combination here is pragmatic, justified by the fact that they operate on different layers, and not a unified philosophical position.

### 4.6 What a reconstruction can claim

The consequences for reconstruction are these. Claims are made for a stated element, at a stated status, on identified evidence. The stopping rule of V6 (§6.10) applies: assert only the highest-level claim the evidence warrants. A whole architecture receives not a single label but a *profile*: for example, that a given number of relations are legally constituted, some of the positions and devices they depend on are instantiated, and operation is not observed. And properties that depend on absence (that a stage is necessary, that no alternative path exists) may be asserted only relative to a declared statement of what was searched, since "not found" is a statement about the search and not about the law.

## 5. The transposition from architectural morphology to regulatory governance

### 5.1 The claim and the objection

The claim is that the analytic structure of configurational architectural theory can be transposed to the legal constitution of governance. The objection is obvious: this is analogy by label. Buildings have walls and doors; laws do not. A transposition worth the name needs conditions, a statement of what corresponds to what, and a statement of what does not.

### 5.2 Correspondences

| Architectural morphology | Regulatory governance (this proposal) | What transfers | What does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space (cell) | **Position**: a legally specified place in relations (organization, office or actor category) | The idea that a unit is defined by its relations to the whole | Physical extension; a position is not a place |
| Access/permeability between spaces | **Qualified relation**: typed, directed, with modality, condition, validity and provenance | Relations, not elements, are the primary analytic data | Symmetry and co-presence: legal relations are directed, normative, of many types, conditional and time-bound |
| Configuration | **Subconfiguration / whole**: a set of relations satisfying a pattern rule | Properties defined by arrangement; emergence | Metric depth; graph measures presuppose adjacency that law does not supply |
| Local vs global structure | **S vs A** | The scale distinction and its guard against over-generalizing | — |
| Door, threshold | **Interface**: an institutional device (register, scheme, platform) through which governing work passes | Treating transitions between domains as analytic objects | Physical function; a device is a legal-institutional entity with a material carrier |
| Genotype (recurrent configurational pattern) | **Named pattern matched to a subconfiguration** (delegated, enhanced self-regulation, accreditation chain, etc.) | Recurrent patterns as configurations, not labels for wholes | The claim that patterns encode cultural principles in the same way |
| Plan / built form / use | **Formal / implemented / operational** architecture; documented / constituted / instantiated / observed | Separate evidence for design, realization and use | The assumption that realization is more reliable than design |
| Boundary of the building | **Boundary of the corpus** (instrument set, jurisdiction, domain, interval, version) | Without a boundary there is no whole | A natural boundary; in law the boundary is a decision and must be declared |

### 5.3 Conditions of disciplined transposition

Four conditions are proposed.

1. **Structure, not measures.** Transfer the logic of part, relation, configuration and boundary, and the attribution of properties to bearers. Do not import indices computed on spatial graphs. Any measure computed on legal relations is a separate step requiring separate justification.
2. **Explicit bearer assignment.** Every property is assigned to a part, a relation, a subconfiguration or the whole, and cannot be moved between them without a stated aggregation rule.
3. **A contrast design.** Architectural claims are tested by holding inventories constant and varying relations, or by varying boundaries and checking sensitivity.
4. **Legal meaning is not delegated to structure.** Structural analysis identifies patterns and positions; interpretation of the law identifies what the relationships are and how much they matter (V6 §6.6: "a relational-configurational approach").

### 5.4 Dis-analogies that matter

Legal relations are normatively constituted: they obtain because a norm says so, and the norm may be conditional, expired, contested or never applied. They are directed and heterogeneous (supervision, reporting, authorization, review, accountability) and so form a multiplex pattern rather than a single graph. They exist in time and have versions. The evidence for them is textual and interpretive; much of it is derived from several provisions rather than read off one. And the material carrier of a relation is not a wall but a set of inscriptions and devices on which it depends.

A tempting heuristic from the architectural side, that a statutory power or duty is a "door" that makes one position accessible to another, is useful only to remind that relations create asymmetric access to governing capacity. It must not be pressed further. The transposition is of the question that configurational theory asks (what follows from the way parts are arranged with respect to one another?) and of its discipline in attributing properties; it is not a claim that governance is space.

## 6. A morphological-relational ontology of regulatory governance architecture

### 6.1 Definition

V6's definition is retained. Our specification is:

> A **regulatory governance architecture** is a *bounded*, *temporally indexed* configuration of legally constituted **positions** and **qualified relations** (with the **institutional devices** that form their interfaces), through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed. Its properties arise from the **arrangement** of the relations and not from an inventory of its parts. It is reconstructed from **material inscriptions**, and its legal constitution, material instantiation and enacted operation are analytically distinct.

The specification adds three things to V6's wording: boundedness and time-indexing (already required by §5.2.3), an explicit part/whole rule, and material anchoring with the three-way separation of constitution, instantiation and operation. It takes nothing away.

### 6.2 Strata

The ontology distinguishes strata of different ontological kinds, together with one transversal dimension and one adjacent layer.

```
 E  EVIDENCE            provisions · versions · statements — the inscriptions
      │   each step is an inference governed by an explicit rule (V6 §6.7)
      ▼
 P  PARTS               Positions;  Institutional devices (a distinct type, not positions)
      ▼
 R  QUALIFIED RELATIONS the reified relation: type · parties · modality · condition · validity
      │                 object · function · mechanism · instrument · authority · discretion ·
      │                 accountability pointer · dependencies · provenance
      ▼
 S  SUBCONFIGURATIONS   local arrangements matched to a pattern rule; may overlap or nest
      ▼
 A  WHOLE               bounded architecture → a profile of whole-level properties

 M  EVIDENTIARY ANCHORING (transversal): which inscription, device or practice supports each
    claim, at which status, with which limitation
 X  ADJACENT LAYER      strategies · governance logics · implemented and operational
                        arrangements · outcomes · sociomaterial enactment
```

**Evidence (E)** comprises provision-level inscriptions with their versions and provenance. A source document is not an actor and not part of the architecture; it is what the architecture is reconstructed from. **Parts (P)** are of two types. A *position* is a legally specified place in relations: an organization, an office, or an actor category; an office is not its holder. A position is a *type* (a generic legal category such as "national competent authority") or an *instance* (a specific institution or office), situated in a jurisdiction and a period; instances are recorded only with their own sources and are never inferred from a generic category, and a type carries the legal multiplicity the text states, if any. An *institutional device* is a juridically constituted entity with its own boundary and rules, such as a register, an accreditation system or a reporting platform. A device is never an actor.

**Relations (R)** are legally constituted connections among positions, reified so that they can carry qualifiers and provenance. The pivotal importance V6 gives to relationships is preserved. A relation's status is one of established, conditional or expressly prohibited; whether it is stated directly or derived from several provisions is recorded; and "not found" is not a relation but a statement about a search. An obligation on one actor does not by itself establish a relation to any other, a point on which V6's rules of inference (§6.7) and the ontology agree.

**Subconfigurations (S)** are sets of relations that satisfy a pattern rule: a verification chain, a delegation triangle, an accountability loop. They are scoped to a function, a stage or a subsystem and may overlap or nest. V6's named architectures (hierarchical, delegated, self-regulation, enforced self-regulation, meta-regulation, enhanced self-regulation, accreditation and assurance chains, networked, polycentric and multilevel; §5.2.2) are treated as patterns matched to subconfigurations, each with its own inference rule, and not as labels for whole laws. This is the formulation V6's own §5.2.3 asks for.

**The whole (A)** is the architecture of an explicitly bounded corpus: instruments, jurisdiction, domain, interval, text version and the rule that fixes membership. A property of "the architecture" is only defined relative to that boundary. The output is a profile of properties and a profile of claim statuses, not a single score or label.

### 6.3 Not every construct is a node

A principal consequence of the strata is that the twelve constructs of V6 do not become twelve kinds of vertex. Positions are the nodes of the actor-position projection. Objects, functions and mechanisms are qualifiers of relations. Authority and discretion are attributes of relations that are also aggregated into distributions over the whole. Accountability is treated below. Strategies are annotations derived from sets of relations and, as V6 itself holds, are orthogonal to architecture. Governance logics belong to an interpretive horizon above the architecture. Instruments take three forms. The first, and default, is a *typed characteristic* of a relation. The second is an *institutional device*, a juridically constituted entity with identity and an operator, rules or a bounded function, supported by the legal source. The third is a *materially instantiated device*, for which evidence beyond the legal prescription is required. A *documentary artefact*, such as a certificate or a published report, is an output or a piece of evidence and not a device. A graph is an analytical projection of the ontology and not the ontology itself: the actor-position graph shows positions as nodes and devices as interfaces on edges; other projections may show devices as nodes or link evidence to elements; each declares what it omits, and none is legal evidence.

### 6.4 A worked case: accountability

The treatment of accountability shows how the ontology operationalizes a construct that is contested. V6 lists accountability both as one of ten relationship types and as an attribute of relationships, and states that reporting alone does not constitute accountability (§4.3). The ontology keeps both roles and links them by reference: one accountability relation exists as a single object, and other relations or arrangements carry a *pointer* to the accountability relations that qualify them, so that no observation is duplicated.

Following the conception in which accountability is a relationship between an actor and a forum, in which the actor has an obligation to explain and justify its conduct (Bovens 2007; bibliographic record and abstract verified, the full text not yet consulted), we distinguish four classes of evidence:

- **Information** — reporting, disclosure or publicity without a duty of justification; never accountability on its own.
- **Answerability** — an identifiable actor-forum relation with a duty of explanation and justification and an institutionalized capacity of questioning, but lacking assessment/judgment capacity or a grounded possibility of consequences.
- **Accountability** — all of these, together with institutionalized assessment/judgment capacity and a possibility of consequences grounded in the legal source.
- **Indeterminate** — an assertion of accountability whose elements cannot be established from the corpus.

Forums may be distributed or public when their relevant capacities are institutionally identifiable; a defined category of persons with a channel and a duty to respond is not excluded merely for being diffuse. Supervision alone does not establish accountability, though it may supply the forum's assessment capacity where a duty to explain is also present. That the capacities are legally constituted does not show that the arrangement operates: exercise is a separate observational claim. The case illustrates the general stance of the ontology: constructs are specified by explicit, source-grounded criteria, with the missing elements recorded when evidence is incomplete.

### 6.5 Material anchoring and external references

Evidentiary anchoring attaches to claims. An evidence link records the claim, the artefact or artefacts, the level of evidence, whether the support is direct or indirect and the limitation. The same artefact may support different claims at different levels. A register extract may support the claim that an entry was recorded on a given date (a limited claim about the act of recording), and may also bear on the claim that the register exists, without supporting any claim about the accuracy or effect of the entry. Artefacts keep their own metadata for authenticity, provenance and date, and an artefact of unverified authenticity cannot raise the status of any claim above documentation.

Legally relevant positions, devices or standards are often constituted outside the immediate boundary of a corpus: in another instrument, in another part of the same instrument, or in a technical standard incorporated by reference. They are represented as typed *external references*, recording the referenced source and its kind (a legal instrument is distinguished from a technical standard or an administrative act, since source type is not the same as legal force), the role of the reference, the status of its verification, whether it lies outside the boundary and is not expanded, and what element depends on it and how. A reference shows legal relevance only; it never demonstrates instantiation or operation; no relations from the referenced source enter the architecture unless a boundary decision includes them. Such references also limit what may be asserted about absence: a claim that a stage is necessary cannot be made if an unresolved external reference with a constitutive dependency touches the path.

Operational-only relationships, such as a practice without a legal basis or a practice departing from a constituted power, are not relations of the legally constituted architecture. They may be recorded in the adjacent layer as observed arrangements, with evidence of observation and an optional reference to the relation they supplement or depart from. Any comparison between formal and operational architecture is then explicit (V6 §6.13).

### 6.6 Properties of parts and of wholes

Four kinds of property are distinguished and not interchanged without a stated rule. *Intrinsic* properties are what the text confers on a position (a power, an assigned function). *Relational* properties belong to a relation (its type, direction, authority, discretion). *Positional* properties belong to a position *within* a configuration and depend on the whole: being a necessary stage, the depth from the rule-making position, substitutability. *Emergent* properties belong to a subconfiguration or the whole and arise from the arrangement of the relations: distributions and structural characteristics. The atomistic and ecological fallacies of V6 are failures to respect these bearers.

V6's ten architectural dimensions can be assigned to bearers. Centralization, public-private allocation, vertical distribution, decision-centre plurality and functional allocation are properties of the whole. Directness and intermediation, self-regulatory autonomy and relational depth are best stated per function or stage, in subconfigurations, and then summarized. Discretion distribution and accountability structure are profiles over relations and positions. The dimensions can further be grouped into five families (composition and distribution; typed relational configuration; authority stratification and distribution; integration and segmentation; interface and boundary permeability); the grouping is a proposal for organizing comparison and not a validated measurement scheme. We propose no aggregate score for any dimension. In particular, discretion is preserved by function, position and qualified relation, in keeping with V6's insistence that discretion is function-specific, and not reduced to a number. Properties that depend on counts of instances (the number of decision centres) are computed only from evidenced instances or reported as bounded by the legal multiplicity the text states.

### 6.7 A contrast: same inventory, different configuration

The discriminating logic can be shown with an invented example (illustrative only, not legal evidence). Let three positions occur in two imagined laws: a supervisory authority X, a verifier V and a firm F, with the same functions in both.

- *Law α.* X accredits V; V verifies F; F reports to X; X may sanction F and V.
- *Law β.* V verifies F; F reports to V; V informs X only in aggregate; X may sanction F only on V's referral; X has no power over V.

The part-level statement "V verifies F" is identical in both. Yet V is a necessary stage between F and the authority in β and is not in α, and X's lines to F differ. No coding of the individual provisions distinguishes the laws; their configurations do. Note that the claim that V is a necessary stage depends on absence (the absence of a direct route from X to F) and may therefore only be asserted for a declared corpus whose coverage has been stated, with unresolved external references listed.

## 7. Implications for relational leximetrics

### 7.1 The relation as the pivotal unit, and what is coded

V6's unit of observation links a provision to the object, the actors, the function, the mechanism and the relationship, supplemented by attributes (§6.3). The ontology preserves this and adds, for each observation, the status of the claims on which it rests. Two things follow. Coders need work only at the level of relations and their qualifiers. Subconfigurations, architectures and their properties are *derived* from coded relations by stated rules, which is what V6's ladder and its warning against assigning architectural labels at the first stage of coding already require (§6.4). And the commitments the ontology makes explicit (types of party, status of a relation, derivation from several provisions, external references) are exactly the fields that distinguish a relation reconstructed from sources from one drawn from a diagram.

### 7.2 How the twelve constructs are specified

| V6 construct | Role in the ontology | Node? |
|---|---|---|
| Actors | Positions (type and instance); roles are relation-relative | Yes |
| Instruments | Typed characteristic of a relation, or a juridically constituted device, or a materially instantiated device | Device: not a position |
| Relationships | Reified qualified relations; the pivotal bridge | Reified |
| Objects, functions, mechanisms | Qualifiers of relations; function, mechanism and instrument remain three separate slots | No |
| Authority, discretion | Relation attributes; distributions over the whole; no scalar aggregate | No |
| Accountability | A relation *and* a pointer qualifying another relation or arrangement; four classes of evidence | No |
| Strategies | Derived annotation of sets of relations; orthogonal to configuration | No |
| Architectures | The bounded whole; named forms as patterns matched to subconfigurations | Whole |
| Governance logics | Interpretive horizon above the architecture | No |

The specification does not alter V6's constructs; it says which kind of thing each is when an architecture is reconstructed. It also leaves V6's insistence intact that its framework is a conceptual map from which research projects select the dimensions relevant to their domain. What the ontology contributes to that selection is a way of saying what depends on what, so that a project coding a subset can state which higher-order claims its evidence supports and which it does not.

### 7.3 A bridge between general and specialised coding

Relational leximetrics is intended to be general; research on regulatory intermediation, for example, has developed specialised databases and codebooks organized around regulator, intermediary and target. A mapping layer between provision-linked coded statements and reified relations could connect the two: each statement produces zero, one or several relations, with many-to-many links between provisions and relations, a flag for direct or derived, and a status. Such a layer would not replace a specialised codebook; the fields it records (actors, actions, recipients, functions) would supply the parties and functions of relations. It makes explicit what V6 calls the pivotal bridge (§6.3). We offer it as a proposal; no existing implementation is claimed.

A related point concerns a codebook variable of the kind that records the relationship between *different* architectures coded for one legal act (single, alternative, sequential, complementary, separate). On the ontology it is a relation among subconfigurations or architectures at the level of an act or corpus, and not a property inside one architecture; it corresponds to V6's recognition that architecture may vary within a statute (§5.2.3). Its exhaustiveness and the exclusivity of its values cannot be assumed. (The variable is taken from an unpublished working codebook of the intermediation project; permission to cite is to be confirmed.)

### 7.4 Frequency, significance and the contrast design

V6 distinguishes frequency from architectural significance and proposes that significance depends on frequency, authority, centrality, exclusivity, sequence, consequence and substitutability, without generating one number (§6.6). The ontology gives this a home: composition (counts) is a descriptor of evidence and positions; significance is a claim about relations or subconfigurations that requires evidence on the other dimensions; and the claims that depend on absence or necessity are subject to the closure rule. The contrast design adds a test of whether an architectural claim depends on configuration or merely on composition.

### 7.5 Lessons from provision-level measurement

Seidl and Kosti (2026) decompose EU legislation into recitals and sub-article provisions and classify them by digital relevance, regulatory tone, regulated actor and rights, using a human-validated ensemble of language models distilled into smaller models, and report agreement between human coders and between humans and the models. They also state two limitations that are directly relevant here: provisions are treated as if of equal legal weight, and the way provisions relate to one another is not known (as read in the project copy of their unpublished manuscript; `[CITATION CHECK]` on any published version). Three lessons follow for relational leximetrics. Granularity is valuable and aggregates upward freely, which suits the nested units V6 proposes. Validation should be reported task by task, since agreement was lower for the more interpretive task (regulated actor) than for the least interpretive (digital relevance), which is consistent with V6's insistence that validation be examined at corresponding levels and not collapsed into a single reliability statistic (§6.13), and with its distinction between relatively direct coding and progressively more interpretive inference (§6.4). And granular classification alone does not reconstruct how provisions relate. The distinctive step on which the present proposal rests is the reconstruction of governance configurations from legally constituted relations, rather than provision-level classification, which is what the authors identify as outstanding. We do not claim that provision-level work cannot develop in this direction; we claim that the ontology specifies what that development has to preserve.

### 7.6 Re-reading V6's illustration

V6's stylized example (§6.15) has four provisions: periodic risk assessment by providers, verification by an accredited auditor, notification by auditors to a supervisory authority, and a power to suspend a provider's authorization. V6 concludes at the configurational level that the provisions establish third-party assurance combined with public supervisory enforcement, and that "accreditation connects the verifier to a wider system of public control", and it carefully stops short of characterizing the whole statute as intermediated. The ontology sharpens one step. The claim that the verifier is connected to public control through accreditation requires a relation between an accrediting position and the verifier, and a relation between a public authority and the accreditor; neither is supplied by the four provisions as stated, which establish verification, notification and suspension of the provider. With an explicit premise (a provision under which a public authority accredits or supervises the auditor) the stronger configurational claim is warranted and the pattern becomes an accreditation chain in the sense of §5.2.2; without it, the warranted claim is the narrower one of third-party verification combined with public enforcement on the provider. The example thereby serves the purpose V6 gives it, which is to show that the intermediate transformations must be explicit and recoverable. We read this as an application of the ontology to the example and not as a criticism of it.

## 8. Contribution, limitations and future empirical validation

### 8.1 Contribution

The article offers four contributions, each modest and each stated so that it can be assessed.

1. **A specification of V6's constructs by ontological role.** It states what kind of entity each of the twelve constructs is in a reconstruction, and which are nodes, qualifiers, attributes, annotations or whole-level properties.
2. **A part/whole attribution rule.** Four kinds of property are distinguished by bearer, and V6's architectural dimensions are assigned to bearers, with a contrast design for testing whether an architectural claim depends on configuration.
3. **A substantive account of materiality as a constraint on reconstruction.** It separates documentary inscription, legal constitution, material instantiation and observed operation, attributes instantiation to positions and devices, represents the dependence of relations on their carriers, and keeps existence distinct from observability.
4. **Operational criteria for contested constructs.** Accountability, instruments and external dependencies are specified with source-grounded criteria and with explicit categories for incomplete evidence.

The article does not claim to have discovered that law constitutes governance architecture, to have shown that architectural properties are measurable, or to have priority over existing research on regulatory intermediation, indirect governance or the architecture of governance regimes. Whether any of that work already supplies a configurational method for legal constitution has not been established; the comparison with Abbott, Levi-Faur and Snidal (2017) and with more recent work on regulatory architectures, which this draft has not completed against the primary texts, is a precondition of any claim of novelty.

### 8.2 Limitations

- **No empirical validation.** The ontology has been checked only for internal consistency against invented cases, by its author, without blinding. This shows that rules were found wanting and amended; it shows nothing about their adequacy on real legislation, or about inter-coder reliability.
- **The transposition is conditional.** The incremental value of configurational theory rests on contrast designs and bearer assignment that remain to be instantiated in real legal text. If V6's dimensions already capture them without remainder, the value is smaller than claimed.
- **Thresholds are unsettled.** The criteria for treating an instrument as a device and for the elements of accountability, including the requirement of a *grounded* possibility of consequences, are judgments that real texts will test.
- **Philosophical pragmatism.** The pairing of critical realism with a limited use of sociomateriality is not a unified metatheory.
- **Reification and formalism.** Positions and relations "constituted by law" are not thereby real social relations. The separation of constitution, instantiation and operation is meant to resist the inference, but a reader may still find the ontology too formalist; the treatment of unwritten and customary constitution is, for now, a flagged exclusion.
- **Citation status.** Much of the theoretical apparatus is cited from the bibliographic record and the author's understanding of the primary texts, not from page-level verification; the literature on the materiality of law has not yet been engaged.
- **Relation to existing coding.** The mapping to a specialised intermediation codebook and the proposed bridge layer are proposals not yet discussed with the codebook's authors.

### 8.3 What would count against the proposal

The proposal would be weakened if: the contrast design cannot be instantiated in real legislation, that is, if no pair of real arrangements has the same positions and functions and different relations bearing on an architectural property; positional claims cannot be warranted because corpora cannot be closed; coders cannot apply the criteria for devices and for accountability with acceptable agreement; or the ten architectural dimensions turn out to exhaust what the ontology adds.

### 8.4 A validation programme

The next task is empirical and should be bounded.

1. **A bounded demonstration** on a cluster of provisions from an existing instrument, for example provisions concerning certification and accreditation in an EU regulation, from the authentic published text, with a declared boundary and an external-reference register.
2. **Blind coding** of a subset by at least two coders, with agreement reported by variable and by level of interpretation, along the lines V6 proposes, and with model-generated and human-coded observations distinguished.
3. **The contrast design** applied to real instruments, to test whether architectural claims differ where inventories coincide and relations differ.
4. **Sensitivity to boundary and to closure**, reporting how properties change with the corpus and what is left undetermined.
5. **Competing classifications** derived from the same relations, to test V6's expectation that alternative thresholds can be examined on the same data.
6. **Extension to implementation** only afterwards, with sociomaterial approaches as a complementary lens on how constituted architectures are enacted.

### 8.5 Conclusion

Governance becomes an architecture, and not merely an arrangement, when it is a bounded configuration whose properties arise from the way legally constituted positions are connected, and when it can be recovered from identifiable sources by a procedure that can be repeated and challenged. Levi-Faur's framework establishes that law builds such arrangements and offers the vocabulary and the inference rules for reconstructing them. This article has argued that the framework gains in precision if the configurational theory of architecture is used to say where properties reside, a realist account of reconstruction is used to say what may be claimed, and a substantive concept of materiality is used to say what supports the claim. Doing so changes little in the constructs and a good deal in how they are used: not every construct is a node, no property is attributed to the wrong scale, and no architecture is called reconstructed that has only been drawn. Whether the proposal earns its place depends on the validation to which it is now to be put.
