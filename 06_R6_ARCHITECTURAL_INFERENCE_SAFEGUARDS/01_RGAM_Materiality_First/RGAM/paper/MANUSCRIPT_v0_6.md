## Abstract

Law does not only prescribe, prohibit and permit conduct; it also constitutes the arrangements through which conduct is governed. Levi-Faur's relational-leximetrics framework gives this proposition a precise form: a governance architecture is the legally constituted configuration through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed among actors and organized through relationships, mechanisms and instruments, and it is an emergent property of that configuration. The framework already supplies emergence, multidimensional properties, rules of inference and a catalogue of fallacies. This article proposes a *disciplinary specification* of that construct. It draws on configurational theory in architecture to clarify the ontological status of constituent elements and the attribution of properties to parts and wholes; it uses critical realism to separate what an architecture is from what a reconstruction may claim; and it develops a substantive concept of materiality that distinguishes documentary inscription, legal constitution, material instantiation and observed operation. Two problems organize the argument. The first is totality: what an architectural whole is, if its organization cannot be read from an inventory of its components. The second is constitution: valid enacted norms can themselves constitute positions, powers, duties and relations in the legal order, and are therefore not equivalent to drawings of an arrangement that must still be built; but norms constitute in different ways, depending on whether they establish, oblige, permit or condition, so the analogy between plan and building fails in an instructive and limited way. We propose an ontology of evidence, positions, qualified relations, subconfigurations, and architectures reconstructed from a bounded documentary corpus, with evidentiary anchoring across all strata, and state implications for relational leximetrics. The proposal is positioned against existing architectural ecosystem modeling of EU regulation (Stratmann et al. 2026) and draws selectively on the architecture/architecture-description distinction of ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022, without claiming conformity to that standard. The argument is conceptual; empirical validation is the next task.

**Keywords:** regulatory governance; governance architecture; relational leximetrics; configurational theory; critical realism; materiality; constitutive norms

## 1. Introduction: when is governance an architecture?

### 1.1 The question

What kind of governance does a law build? Levi-Faur (2026, §1) puts the question to comparative legal research, which has developed sophisticated ways of comparing what laws require, how much they cover and how stringent they are, but has far fewer tools for comparing how laws organize the work of governing. The intuition behind the question is familiar from regulatory scholarship. Two statutes may impose similar substantive requirements while one relies on direct public inspection, another on firms' own risk-management systems, and a third on market access conditional on certification by accredited private bodies. The difference between them is not located in any one rule, actor or instrument; it lies in how these are arranged.

V6, the draft on which this article builds, gives the intuition a definition and a method. A governance architecture is "the legally constituted configuration through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed among actors and organized through relationships, mechanisms and instruments" (Levi-Faur 2026, §5.2). It is "an emergent property of a configuration" that "cannot normally be inferred from the presence of a single instrument, relationship or actor" (§5.2); it is best understood multidimensionally rather than as a set of mutually exclusive types (§5.2); and "counts identify composition; configurations identify organization" (§6.6). The method, relational leximetrics, takes a legally constituted relationship as its pivotal empirical object and moves from legal text through institutional statements, relationships and configurations to architectures and regimes under explicit rules of inference, a stopping rule and a catalogue of cross-level fallacies (§§6.2–6.10). The twelve constructs are arranged in three levels: objects, actors, functions, mechanisms, instruments and relationships; authority, discretion and accountability as relational attributes; and strategies, architectures and governance logics as higher-order constructions (§§3–5).

### 1.2 The gap, stated precisely

V6 is not silent on emergence, on the difference between composition and organization, on the bearers of properties, or on the limits of aggregation. It states emergence, lists architectural dimensions, distinguishes the units of coding, analysis and inference, and names the fallacies that follow from confusing them. The gap this article addresses is therefore narrower than a gap of omission. V6 presents these distinctions as parts of a methodological apparatus; what it does not undertake, and is not intended to undertake, is to ground them in a disciplinary theory of configuration and to draw out their *ontological* consequences. Doing so raises three questions.

1. **Ontological roles.** What kind of thing is each construct when an architecture is reconstructed? An actor, a function and a mechanism are plainly not the same kind of entity, yet a network drawing treats them alike if each becomes a vertex.
2. **Bearers of properties.** Where does a property reside: in a part, a relationship, a local arrangement or the whole? The atomistic and ecological fallacies presuppose an answer.
3. **Warrant of reconstruction.** What entitles a researcher to describe an architecture as empirically reconstructed rather than designed, projected or merely drawn, and how does that relate to the formal, implemented and operational architectures V6 distinguishes (§6.13)?

The risk in the word itself is also real. "Architecture" is often used as an umbrella label for any non-trivial arrangement of governing actors, and used so it explains little. Moreover, published scholarship has already moved well beyond the label: Stratmann et al. (2026) propose *architectural ecosystem modeling* for EU regulation and visualize the institutional structures associated with distinct AI Act regimes. The present proposal does not claim to originate the architectural depiction of legislation, actor–relationship mapping, or multi-level regulatory visualization. Its question is narrower: how to specify property bearers, legal constitution and evidentiary warrant so that the reconstructed architecture is not confused with a particular diagram. Their methodological choices must be assessed against their own purpose, rather than treated as a deficiency merely because their unit of analysis differs from ours (see §3.5).

### 1.3 Thesis, research question and propositions

The proposed contribution is a disciplinary specification of the architectural construct already developed in relational leximetrics, drawing on configurational theory to clarify the ontological status of constituent elements, the attribution of architectural properties, and the conditions under which legally constituted configurations may be empirically reconstructed.

The research question is:

> *How can a materially anchored and configurational ontology extend Levi-Faur's relational leximetrics, enabling empirically verifiable reconstruction of regulatory governance architecture while distinguishing properties of constituent parts from those of the architectural whole?*

The argument advances four theoretical propositions, which the conclusion revisits. They are propositions of a conceptual argument and not hypotheses to be confirmed by data.

- **P1.** A configuration has properties that cannot be identified from an inventory of its components (§§2–3).
- **P2.** Configurational theory in architecture supplies resources for distinguishing intrinsic, relational, positional and global properties, and thereby for locating the bearers of the properties V6 treats as architectural (§§3, 6).
- **P3.** An empirical reconstruction must distinguish legal constitution, material instantiation and observed operation, and the legal case differs from the built case because enacted norms can themselves constitute legal entities and relations, according to their type (§4).
- **P4.** A morphological ontology can complement relational leximetrics without replacing its substantive categories (§§6–7).

### 1.4 Method of theoretical construction

The article is a theoretical essay with an ontological proposal and methodological implications. It is not a systematic review and reports no empirical study. Its operations were: (i) a conceptual reconstruction of V6; (ii) the derivation of criteria for choosing supporting theory from V6's own problem; (iii) comparison of candidate frameworks against the criteria; (iv) delimitation of correspondences and dis-analogies in the transposition from architecture to governance; (v) specification of an ontology; (vi) targeted positioning against a close published legal-architecture application and an architecture-description standard, based on the two supplied analytical fichamentos and verified public source descriptions, not full critical readings of both primary texts; and (vii) internal consistency checks on invented cases, performed within an AI-assisted development process under the author's direction, by the same process that formulated the rules, without blinding. The last step tested the coherence of the rules; it is not an empirical test of the proposal and does not substitute for one.

### 1.5 Scope

Morphology is the principal analytical object; materiality is the empirical anchor; sociomateriality, the study of how arrangements are enacted in practice, is a complementary perspective and not the object of study. The article treats Stratmann et al. (2026) as a directly relevant methodological antecedent and ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 as a selective vocabulary for architectural description. It does not claim priority over existing work on regulatory intermediation, indirect governance or the "architecture" of governance regimes; where such literatures bear on the argument they are named and their verification status is recorded. The plan is as follows: Section 2 develops the disciplinary concept of architecture; Section 3 presents configurational theory and justifies its selection; Section 4 turns to critical realism, materiality and the problem of reconstruction; Section 5 examines the transposition to governance; Section 6 proposes the ontology; Section 7 draws implications for relational leximetrics; Section 8 states the contribution, limits and validation programme.

## 2. Architecture as a disciplinary concept

### 2.1 Three senses of architecture

In the discipline of architecture the word is used in at least three senses that are routinely distinguished and routinely run together. In the first, architecture is an *artefact*: the building, its fabric and its form. In the second, it is a *design*: an intention to organize, expressed in drawings, specifications and codes before and apart from construction. In the third, it is a *configuration*: the way spaces and the relations of access and separation among them are arranged, so that the building supports some patterns of encounter, movement and avoidance and impedes others. The three are connected, since a design is realized in an artefact whose configuration is then inhabited, but they come apart in practice.

The configurational sense is the one at issue for governance. Hillier's work argues that what is distinctive in architecture as a field of knowledge is the configuration of spatial relations and its social consequences, not the object as such or the designer's intention (Hillier 1996; `[CITATION CHECK]`). Levi-Faur's definition is configurational in this sense: it places architecture in how governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are *distributed and organized*, and not in the mere presence of actors or instruments.

### 2.2 The part–whole problem in the architectural tradition

An article that claims its contribution from the discipline of architecture must show how the discipline has treated the problem of parts and wholes, and why the treatment is relevant to the regulatory object. Three strands are selected, each because it answers a specific problem for the construct, and not as a catalogue of names. Their characterizations are provisional and require verification against the primary texts (`[CITATION CHECK]` throughout this subsection).

*Overlap and decomposition: Alexander.* Alexander (1965) contrasts two abstract structures for organizing the units of a city. In a tree, for any two units either one is wholly contained in the other or they are wholly disjoint; in a semilattice, units may overlap, subject to the axiom that when two overlapping units belong to the collection their common part also belongs to it. He argues that deliberately designed ("artificial") cities tend to be trees whereas spontaneously grown ("natural") cities are semilattices. (The tree/semilattice definitions and this thesis were checked against a web-hosted copy of the essay; the original publication details and pagination were not.) The problem this raises for governance is direct: if an instrument is decomposed into governance arrangements, the decomposition need not be a partition. One position may belong to several arrangements, and one arrangement may nest within another. This is the problem V6 recognizes when it says that mixed and overlapping architectures coexist within a single law (§5.2.3). We take over the problem and not the axiom: Alexander's semilattice axiom requires that the intersection of two units itself be a unit, a closure condition that we do not assume for overlapping subconfigurations of governance.

*Levels of control: Habraken.* Habraken's separation of supports from infill, with different actors controlling different levels of a built environment (Habraken 1972, from the 1961 Dutch original; 1998), raises the problem of how decision capacity is distributed among nested levels. It speaks to V6's dimensions of vertical distribution, centralization and discretion distribution, and to the question of which party controls which level of an arrangement. Its use here is limited to this problem.

*Configuration and its social logic: Hillier and Hanson.* The tradition associated with space syntax treats the relation of each space to all others as the unit of analysis and derives properties of positions and wholes from it (Hillier and Hanson 1984). It is the strand that most directly addresses the problem of properties that belong to a position because of the whole.

A genealogical note: the Brazilian line of configurational research, associated with Frederico de Holanda, whose doctoral work on Brasília was supervised by Hillier (Holanda 2002), is part of the intellectual background the author acknowledges. It is acknowledged as genealogy and not used as a source for the argument, since the argument rests only on the texts it actually mobilizes.

### 2.3 From label to claim

Used as a label, "architecture" is cheap. Used as a *claim*, it carries conditions, and these must be stated carefully, because two kinds of condition are easily confused: conditions on the *object* and conditions on the *knowledge* of it.

| Conditions on the object (what an architecture is) | Conditions on knowledge (what a reconstruction requires) |
|---|---|
| **Relational organization:** the parts are defined by qualified relations among them, not by an inventory | **Identifiable referents:** the claim names the inscriptions, devices or practices through which the arrangement can be investigated |
| **Differentiable constituents:** positions and relations can be told apart and typed | **An explicit procedure of reconstruction** from sources to claims |
| **Emergent properties:** properties of the arrangement that are not properties of any part | **Traceable and contestable evidence** for each step |
| **Delimitability:** the arrangement has a boundary that can be drawn | **A declared boundary:** corpus, jurisdiction, domain, interval and version, and the reach of the inferences |

The left-hand column states what it is for governance to be an architecture; the right-hand column states what a researcher must be able to show to claim that an architecture has been *reconstructed*. The second is an epistemological condition. An architecture may exist without our having evidence sufficient to reconstruct it; recoverability is not a condition of its existence (Section 4). Labels fail both columns: applied to a loose cluster of institutions with no boundary and no stated relations, "architecture" adds nothing to "arrangement".

### 2.4 Documentary boundary and architectural boundary

A complete legal instrument is a defensible *documentary* boundary: a fixed text with a date, a version and an authenticated source. It is not thereby an architectural boundary, and one regulation does not necessarily correspond to one architecture. Five things must be kept apart.

1. **The documentary corpus (W):** the instrument or set of instruments, fixed in version and time.
2. **Legally identified constituents and qualified relations** found in W: positions, normative assignments, devices and relations.
3. **Potentially overlapping subconfigurations (S1…Sn)**, defined by pattern rules over those relations. A subconfiguration may connect provisions from different chapters; the editorial structure of an instrument (titles, chapters, sections) does not determine its architectural structure.
4. **Architectures (A1…Am)** reconstructed from the subconfigurations and relations by explicit inferential criteria. Their number, boundaries and types are not fixed before the relations have been examined, and they need not be mutually exclusive, in keeping with V6's multidimensional understanding (§§5.2, 5.2.3).
5. **The resulting multidimensional architectural profile** of the instrument.

Selecting a corpus is therefore not evidence of a single unified architecture. W is where reconstruction starts, not where it ends. This is V6's container fallacy (§6.8) restated: that provisions sit in one act does not mean they constitute one architecture. Emergent properties belong to the subconfigurations and architectures that are reconstructed, not to W as such. A further distinction concerns representation: the legally constituted arrangement is not identical with the scholarly *architecture description* or with any of its visual views. ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 explicitly separates an entity's architecture from the architecture description that expresses it; it does not establish what law constitutes (developed in §5.5).

### 2.5 Neighbouring concepts

Architecture must be distinguished from concepts regulatory scholars use close by. *Structure* is more general and does not imply a bounded whole. *System* implies functional interdependence that may or may not be configurational. *Design* names intention, which in law may be recorded in preparatory materials but is not the arrangement the law constitutes. *Regime* names a wider and more variably bounded set of rules and institutions; V6 reserves it for the sixth step of its inference ladder, where the boundary must be decided. *Organization* is typically an actor and appears inside architectures as a part. The term has also travelled into systems engineering, enterprise architecture and the study of the "architecture" of international governance. This article draws selectively on the architecture-description vocabulary of ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 and explicitly compares a close regulatory-ecosystem application by Stratmann et al. (2026), but does not treat either as a substantive theory of normative emergence. A fuller comparison of governance-architecture literatures remains necessary before journal submission (Section 8).

### 2.6 What the disciplinary concept supplies

An object (the configuration), a requirement (a bounded whole), a logic (parts defined by their relations to other parts; properties emerging at the level of arrangement), the possibility of overlap among sub-arrangements and distribution of control among levels, and a discipline of keeping design, built form and use apart. Section 4 shows that the last element needs revision for law. The next section turns to the theory that has developed the logic of configuration most fully.

## 3. Hillier and Hanson: configurational theory and its comparative justification

### 3.1 Configuration as the relation of each part to the whole

Hillier and Hanson's *The Social Logic of Space* (1984) develops what became known as space syntax, a theory and method for describing the spatial organization of buildings and settlements and relating it to social patterns. Four ideas are of interest here, stated in our own words and subject to verification (`[CITATION CHECK]` for each).

First, space is not a neutral container. The layout of spaces is itself a social and cultural product that carries and shapes patterns of co-presence, encounter and control. Second, the unit of analysis is the *configuration*: not the set of connections as such, but the relations among spaces *considered with respect to all the others*. A given space is deep or shallow, integrated or segregated, because of how it stands to the whole, and a change elsewhere in the system changes its properties although nothing about the space itself has changed. Third, the method represents arrangements through graphs of access relations and derives measures of depth and integration, so that two arrangements built from the same rooms can be shown to differ. Fourth, *Ideas Are in Things* (Hillier, Hanson and Graham 1987) applies the approach to detect recurrent configurational patterns, "genotypes", in sets of houses, using observable configuration as evidence about underlying cultural principles.

The distinction between a configuration and a graph is useful here. A graph is a representation: a set of nodes and edges. A configuration, in the sense of the tradition, is a *theory of dependence*: the properties of each part are functions of its relations to every other part, so that no part can be characterized in isolation. Representing a configuration as a graph is one way to study it. Network analysis can compute positional and structural properties of any graph, and configurational reasoning is not owned by one discipline: it also appears in network science and in other traditions. What the architectural tradition contributes is a particular disciplinary understanding of part–whole dependence, positional properties and emergent organization, developed around built form and its social consequences, together with a practice of attributing properties to the appropriate scale. The reason for drawing on it here is not that other approaches cannot study such properties, but that it offers a worked-out treatment of the problem V6 poses.

### 3.2 Why configuration matters for the V6 construct

V6's definition asserts emergence and states the fallacies of neglecting it. What the construct does not undertake is the identification and attribution of emergent and positional properties. Consider V6's illustrative example: a regime requiring that assessments be verified by an accredited auditor who notifies a supervisory authority, which may suspend a provider's authorization (Levi-Faur 2026, §6.15). Each provision can be coded as a separate observation. That the auditor is "consequential to the operation of the regulatory process" is not a property of the auditor's provision; it is a property of the auditor's position in the whole and depends on what else the law provides. A configurational vocabulary makes this explicit. The same part-level statement ("the auditor verifies the provider") can occur in arrangements in which the auditor is a necessary stage and in arrangements in which it is not, and the architectural claim differs accordingly.

### 3.3 Comparative justification

A framework for specifying V6 should be chosen against explicit criteria, and the criteria should come from V6's own problem and not from the framework. Nine are suggested by the construct and its method.

| C2 | Part/whole separation: a place to say which properties belong to which scale | atomistic and ecological fallacies (§6.8) |
| C3 | Qualified and typed relations | relations as pivotal and typed (§6.3) |

The result is a division of labour: V6 supplies the substance; critical realism supplies the ontology and epistemology of reconstruction; the configurational tradition supplies the part/whole structure; institutional grammar feeds the statement level; legal-ontology practice supplies modelling discipline; Stratmann et al. establish direct regulatory-modeling precedent; ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 supplies a selective descriptive vocabulary; sociomateriality is a complementary lens. Configurational theory is chosen for the structural task because it is the candidate most directly addressing C1 and C2 as a theory of dependence among parts and wholes, and not as a computational technique alone. The last row of the table shows why the choice requires combination: what the tradition does not resolve is precisely what V6's regulatory theory and the realist and materiality frame must supply.

### 3.4 What configurational theory adds beyond an inventory of V6's categories

The claim of incremental value must be stated so that it can fail. Five contributions are suggested; none is a discovery about governance.

1. **A treatment of emergence.** The tradition treats the properties of a position as determined by its relations to all others. This yields *positional* properties (being a necessary stage; depth from the rule-making position) that cannot be read from a part's own text.
2. **A contrast design.** If arrangements with the same constituents and different relations differ in their properties, holding the composition constant and varying the wiring is a way of testing whether an architectural claim depends on configuration or only on composition.
3. **Local arrangement and global structure.** The distinction maps onto subconfigurations and the whole and disciplines generalization from local findings.
4. **Boundary and threshold as analytic objects.** The tradition treats the points at which access passes from one space to another as significant. Their counterpart in governance is the *interface*: a legally constituted point of transition between positions, such as a duty to notify, a designation, an approval or an authorization. An interface is not necessarily a separate institutional device. Many are simply relations; some are mediated by a register, a platform, a certification system or another autonomous device, and only then does a device belong to the picture.
5. **An epistemological parallel.** *Ideas Are in Things* infers underlying principles from observable configuration. This parallels, without being identical to, V6's route from legal text to configurations. It is a parallel in the structure of the inference and not a claim that space syntax and relational leximetrics are the same technique.

The claim of incremental value *fails* if the contrast design cannot be instantiated in real legal text, or if V6's ten architectural dimensions already capture contributions 1 to 4 without remainder.

### 3.5 A close prior application: architectural ecosystem modeling of the AI Act

Stratmann et al. (2026) provide an especially relevant antecedent for the proposed application of architectural thinking to EU regulation. Their published study introduces *architectural ecosystem modeling* as a visualization-based method to represent the institutional structures embedded in the AI Act, including actors across EU and national levels, distributed duties, oversight arrangements and relationships of institutional interdependence. Their illustrations distinguish the regulatory regimes for high-risk AI systems and general-purpose AI models. Accordingly, representing a legal act as an architecture, abstracting actors and legal relationships, or producing multi-level diagrams must not be claimed as innovations of the present article (Stratmann et al. 2026).

The relationship is complementary but also analytically challenging. Their work foregrounds the production and legal-interpretive usefulness of architectural ecosystem *models* for selected regulatory regimes. The present proposal asks an additional ontological and inferential question: when may a modeled relation, a subconfiguration or a whole-level property be asserted as a property of the *legally constituted arrangement* rather than of the visualization? This requires explicit claim bearers, legal modality and conditions, boundaries, external references and differentiated evidence for constitution, material establishment and observed operation. These proposed extensions must be assessed in practice; no comparative superiority over Stratmann et al.'s method is asserted. Its regime-focused selection is appropriate to its stated visual-analytical purpose. Our planned whole-instrument GDPR exercise instead adopts a fixed documentary boundary before identifying internal configurations, because its question concerns the range and overlap of architectural properties within that instrument.

The second comparison is supplied by ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022: its architecture/description distinction gives a disciplined vocabulary for avoiding the conflation of the underlying regulatory object, a particular graph and a multi-view description. The standard is an architecture-*description* standard, not a theory of normative emergence or a legal coding protocol. For this article, the three levels of contribution are therefore: Stratmann et al. as a directly related application in regulatory-ecosystem visualization; ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 as a reference for plurality and consistency of descriptions; and Hillier and Hanson as the configurational strand from which the part–whole proposition is developed.

## 4. Bhaskar, materiality and the epistemological problem of reconstruction

### 4.1 The problem

Reconstructing a governance architecture from legal sources is an inferential task: the researcher reads inscriptions and infers statements, relations, configurations and finally an architecture. Two errors threaten it. The first treats what is directly countable in the text as all there is. The second treats the architecture as merely the analyst's construction. V6's fallacies point at both: the atomistic, frequency and container fallacies are errors of the first kind; the functional and implementation fallacies treat a formal assignment as a performed function. What is needed is an account of what an architecture is, in what sense it exists, and what a reconstruction may claim about it. The three operations of the article are therefore distinct: the architectural operation defines configuration and position; the ontological operation says what the constituents and their properties are; the epistemological operation says under what conditions an architecture can be said to be reconstructed. Critical realism is introduced for the last two, not as another theory of architecture.

### 4.2 Critical realism as ontological and epistemological frame

Bhaskar's critical realism (1975, 1979) offers an account in outline as follows `[CITATION CHECK]`. Reality is stratified: there are structures and powers (the real), the events that occur when they are exercised (the actual), and the experiences and observations through which both are known (the empirical). What exists is not reduced to what is observed; to do so is the "epistemic fallacy". Knowledge is fallible and socially produced: the objects of inquiry are independent of it (the intransitive dimension) while theories are its products (the transitive dimension). Wholes can have emergent properties that their parts lack. For the social world, social structures depend on human activity and are yet not reducible to it; Archer's analytic dualism develops the point (Archer 1995; `[CITATION CHECK]`).

This frame licenses speaking of an architecture as something that may exist whether or not it has been recorded or observed, while requiring that claims about it be warranted and revisable; it gives a philosophical basis for the part/whole distinction; and it offers a loose correspondence with V6's tripartite distinction, which must not be pressed: V6's three architectures are not the three realist strata. One terminological hazard: Bhaskar's "generative mechanism" is not V6's "mechanism", a means of governing such as command, disclosure or accreditation. Below, "mechanism" has V6's meaning.

### 4.3 Where the architectural analogy breaks: constitutive norms

Section 2 introduced the distinction between design, built form and use. It is natural to map it onto V6's formal, implemented and operational architectures: the text as a drawing, the organization as the building, practice as use. The mapping is useful for the structure of evidence and misleading for the ontology, and the way it misleads is instructive.

A drawing represents a design. Its existence confers no building: nothing is built until someone builds it, and the drawing may be perfectly authentic while the building does not exist. An enacted norm is not merely a representation. Valid law can itself constitute legal positions, powers, duties and relations in the legal order: this is the performative or constitutive character of norms, a theme of the theory of institutional facts (Searle 1995; `[CITATION CHECK]`, bibliographic record unchecked). Where a norm does so, the legally constituted arrangement is not an unbuilt design; it is established in one medium, the normative-legal, independently of anything established in another, the organizational and material.

That claim must not be overgeneralized. Norms do not all constitute in the same way, and a narrative that treated every statutory mention of an entity or device as bringing it into legal being would confuse an obligation to establish something with its establishment. Four cases are distinguished, using an invented register as the example.

| Type of norm | Invented example | What is legally constituted | What is not thereby constituted |
|---|---|---|---|
| **N1 Direct establishment** | "A National Register of Verifiers is hereby established." | The register as a legal entity or device, from the date the norm takes effect | Its material establishment; its use |
| **N2 Obligation to establish** | "The Minister shall establish a register of verifiers within twelve months." | An obligation of the Minister (and a relation, if a forum or counterparty is identified) | The register itself: a duty to create it is not evidence that it has been legally established |
| **N3 Permission or competence** | "The Minister may establish a register of verifiers." | A competence or permission | The register; not even a duty to establish it |
| **N4 Condition on legal effect or operation** | "The register is established when the implementing act enters into force." | A conditional constitution, effective for the interval after the condition is met | The register before the condition is met |

N1 may itself be subject to N4-type conditions (dates of entry into force or of application). Drafting cues matter, may not survive translation, and must be checked on the authentic text; ambiguous wording is a matter for adjudication and not for inference.

Four consequences follow.

1. **Formal architecture is neither uniformly constituted nor uniformly a projection.** What a norm constitutes depends on its type. Where it establishes (N1), V6's formal legal architecture contains the entity as constituted; where it only obliges, permits or conditions (N2–N4), the formal architecture contains the duty, the competence or the conditional norm, and the entity remains, in the legal order, a required, permitted or contingent one.
2. **"Unbuilt" has two senses.** An entity may be unestablished in the legal order (N2–N4 before the constituting act) and, separately, unestablished in the organizational order (N1 with no system behind it). The two must be kept apart.
3. **Materiality differs by medium.** For legal constitution it concerns the inscriptions that carry the norm and the evidence that they are authentic, enacted and in force. For establishment and exercise it concerns offices, devices, infrastructures and practices.
4. **Absence of evidence is not non-existence.** That the corpus yields no evidence that an entity has been legally constituted, or materially established, limits the researcher's claim; it does not show that nothing exists. The entity may have been constituted by an instrument outside the corpus (an external reference), or established in practice, or simply be unevidenced. The ontological question and the evidential question remain separate.

Two cautions. First, Searle's account of institutional facts and the critical realism of Section 4.2 are not assumed to be reconcilable; the point about constitutive norms is made on its own, and is compatible with a stratified view in which the structures of the legal order are real. Second, the converse also holds in principle: a norm or arrangement may be constituted without an enacted text (by custom or constitutional convention), and an organizational arrangement may operate without legal constitution. These cases lie outside reconstruction from enacted sources and are flagged, not denied.

### 4.4 Materiality as a substantive concern

If an architecture can exist independently of its observation, why require *material* referents for a reconstruction? The answer lies in what kind of thing a legally constituted architecture is. It is not a physical object and it is not an idea in the analyst's mind. It is a set of institutional arrangements that exist because they have been inscribed in authoritative texts, constituted with legal effect, established as offices, registers and procedures, and, in some cases, enacted in practice. Each is a different relation between the arrangement and the things that carry it. Materiality, as used here, is the theoretical concern with how a legally constituted architecture finds expression and support in identifiable inscriptions and institutional devices.

Materiality is not a synonym for a building, for physical adjacency, for the existence of a PDF, or for implementation. Digital inscriptions and artefacts have material infrastructures too, and an enacted text, an official register and a platform are all material in the relevant sense. An architecture can have material expression in its constituting texts while its implementation is entirely unknown.

Four things are distinguished that are often merged.

- **Documentary inscription.** An authentic, versioned document or trace exists, even if it is a draft, a repealed text or a non-binding guidance.
- **Legal constitution.** A norm that is valid and applicable for the stated version and period constitutes an entity, power, duty or relation in the legal order. *Which* of these it constitutes depends on its type (§4.3): a norm obliging the establishment of a register constitutes the obligation, not the register.
- **Material establishment (instantiation).** The position or device that has been legally constituted exists as an established entity with identifiable carriers: the office has been filled, the register exists and is accessible, the platform exists. This is evidence of *establishment*. It is not evidence that the entity performs its regulatory function or is used.
- **Observed exercise (operation).** The exercise of a function is observed: use, compliance, appropriation. This is a separate evidential matter and the object of the adjacent layer of enactment.

The distinction between establishment and exercise is analytical and carries no presumed sequence. A register may exist and never be consulted; a function may be exercised informally before any device is established.

Two axes must not be confused. The first is the **legal status** of a norm in the legal order for an interval: in force, not yet applicable, suspended, expired or repealed, or conditional on a further act. It is a matter of law, and is evidenced by legal sources such as publication and entry-into-force provisions. The second is the **epistemic status** of the researcher's claim about an entity, relation or architecture. Five epistemic statuses are distinguished: conceptually projected; documented; legal constitution evidenced; material establishment evidenced; exercise evidenced. They attach to individual claims, each needs its own evidence, none is inferred from another, and they are not a life cycle: a practice may precede a formal basis, and a norm may lapse while a practice persists. "Documented" and "legal constitution evidenced" are therefore not values of legal status. A repealed text is documented and has the legal status *repealed*; a claim that an entity is legally constituted is a claim whose epistemic status depends on evidence of the norm's validity and applicability. Applied to the register: a draft bill is documented, its legal status is "not enacted", and no legal constitution is evidenced. A mandate to establish a platform that has not been built (N2) is documented and its legal status is "in force", but what is legally constituted is the obligation, not the platform. A direct establishment of a register with no system behind it (N1) can have its legal constitution evidenced while its material establishment is not. An accessible PDF shows documentation, and, with evidence of enactment and force, legal constitution of whatever the norm constitutes; it shows nothing about whether the offices it creates are filled.

Materiality also bears on *relations*. A legally constituted relation (say, that verifiers must record their results in a national register) *depends* on the entities through which it exists or is exercised. A relation has no body of its own, so instantiation is attributed to the positions and devices on which it depends, and the relation carries a record of its support dependencies and any gaps. If practice occurs without the device, the evidence of practice stands and the gap is reported; the dependency is an expectation and not a gate. This is the sense in which materiality exceeds provenance: provenance says where a datum came from, whereas materiality concerns what carries the constituted relation so that it can exist as law, be exercised and be shown.

### 4.5 Existence and reconstruction: two theses

The apparent tension between realism and a materiality requirement dissolves once two theses are separated. The *ontological* thesis is weak: legal-institutional architecture is not free-floating; it is constituted by socially real structures that depend on inscriptions, offices, registers, practices and infrastructures, and "material" does not mean "physical". The *epistemic* thesis is stricter: a claim that an architecture has been *empirically reconstructed* must name the material referents and evidence through which it can be investigated, at the status claimed. The second governs the warrant of the claim and not the existence of the thing. An operational arrangement may exist undocumented, and absence of evidence that an entity has been established does not show that none has. When a defensible material referent is lacking, the researcher's claim has the epistemic status of a projection, an ideal type or a design hypothesis: it may be true of the world, but it is not yet a reconstruction and must not be presented unqualified as one.

*Evidentiary anchoring* is the methodological counterpart of materiality: the records, attached to claims, by which the dependencies and statuses just described are supported. The two are not synonyms. Materiality is a theoretical concept about how architectures are carried; evidentiary anchoring is the research practice that makes claims about that carrying checkable.

### 4.6 Sociomateriality as complementary lens

The sociomateriality literature, associated with Orlikowski (2007) and Orlikowski and Scott (2008), is generally read as a relational ontology in which the social and the material are constitutively entangled and entities lack inherent properties prior to enactment (`[CITATION CHECK]`). Leonardi (2012) discusses what the terms materiality and sociomateriality mean and how they differ, and argues for an approach that keeps the social and the material analytically distinct, closer to a stratified realism (`[CITATION CHECK]`). The two are not a consensus and should not be cited as one. Our use is deliberately narrow: sociomateriality applies to the layer of enactment, how configurations, once constituted and established, are used and transformed; it is not applied to the constitutive core and not reduced to implementation as a concept. A critical-realist frame and a constitutive-entanglement ontology are not jointly consistent as metatheories, so the combination is pragmatic, justified by their operating on different layers, and not a unified philosophical position. The same holds for the other frameworks used here: Bhaskar's realism, the configurational tradition, Searle's account of institutional facts and sociomaterial approaches serve different tasks and are not merged into a single metatheory; their compatibility is not assumed.

### 4.7 What a reconstruction can claim

Claims are made for a stated element, at a stated status, on identified evidence. V6's stopping rule applies: assert only the highest-level claim the evidence warrants (§6.10). A whole architecture receives not a single label but a *profile*: for example, that a given number of relations are legally constituted, that some of the positions and devices they depend on are established, and that operation is not observed. And properties that depend on absence (that a stage is necessary, that no alternative path exists) may be asserted only relative to a declared statement of what was searched, since "not found" is a statement about the search and not about the law.

## 5. The transposition from architectural morphology to regulatory governance

### 5.1 The claim and the objection

The claim is that the analytic structure of configurational architectural theory can be transposed to the legal constitution of governance. The objection is that this is analogy by label: buildings have walls and doors, laws do not. A transposition worth the name needs conditions, a statement of what corresponds to what, and a statement of what does not.

### 5.2 Correspondences

| Architectural morphology | Regulatory governance (this proposal) | What transfers | What does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space (cell) | **Position**: a legally specified place in relations | A unit is defined by its relations to the whole | Physical extension |
| Access/permeability between spaces | **Qualified relation**: typed, directed, with modality, condition, validity and provenance | Relations, not elements, are the primary analytic data | Symmetry and co-presence: legal relations are directed, normative, heterogeneous, conditional and time-bound |
| Configuration | **Subconfiguration / whole**: a set of relations satisfying a pattern rule | Properties defined by arrangement; emergence | Metric depth; graph measures presuppose adjacency that law does not supply |
| Local vs global structure; overlapping sub-arrangements | **S vs A**; overlap and nesting of S | Scale distinction; overlap (Alexander) | — |
| Levels of control | **Distribution of authority and discretion** among positions and levels | Attention to who controls which level (Habraken) | — |
| Door, threshold | **Interface**: a legally constituted point of transition between positions (a notification, designation, approval), sometimes mediated by a separate institutional device | Transitions between domains as analytic objects | Physical function; not every transition requires a register, platform or other autonomous device |
| Genotype (recurrent configurational pattern) | **Named pattern matched to a subconfiguration** | Recurrent patterns as configurations, not labels for wholes | The claim that patterns encode cultural principles in the same way |
| Design → built form → use | **Legal constitution → material establishment → observed exercise** | Separate evidence for each | The idea that the first step is always a representation: some norms constitute entities and relations, others only oblige, permit or condition (§4.3) |
| Boundary of the building | **Boundary of the documentary corpus (W)** and, separately, of each reconstructed architecture (instrument set, jurisdiction, domain, interval, version) | Without a boundary there is no whole | A natural boundary; the documentary boundary does not fix the architectural one, and one instrument may yield several architectures (§2.4) |

### 5.3 Conditions of disciplined transposition

1. **Structure, not measures.** Transfer the logic of part, relation, configuration and boundary and the attribution of properties to bearers. Do not import indices computed on spatial graphs; any measure computed on legal relations is a separate step requiring separate justification.
2. **Explicit bearer assignment.** Every property is assigned to a part, a relation, a subconfiguration or the whole, and cannot be moved between them without a stated aggregation rule.
3. **A contrast design.** Architectural claims are tested by holding composition constant and varying relations, or by varying boundaries and checking sensitivity.
4. **Legal meaning is not delegated to structure.** Structural analysis identifies patterns and positions; interpretation of the law identifies what the relationships are and how much they matter (V6 §6.6: a "relational-configurational approach").

### 5.4 Dis-analogies that matter

Legal relations are normatively constituted: they obtain because a norm says so, and the norm may be conditional, expired, contested or never applied. They are directed and heterogeneous (supervision, reporting, authorization, review, accountability), so they form a multiplex pattern and not a single graph. They exist in time and have versions. The evidence for them is textual and interpretive, and much of it is derived from several provisions. The material carrier of a relation is not a wall but a set of inscriptions and devices on which it depends. And, as §4.3 shows, some norms are constitutive of entities and relations while others only oblige, permit or condition. A tempting heuristic, that a statutory power or duty is a "door" making one position accessible to another, is useful only to remind that relations create asymmetric access to governing capacity. The transposition is of the question configurational theory asks (what follows from the way parts are arranged with respect to one another?) and of its discipline in attributing properties; it is not a claim that governance is space.

### 5.5 An architecture is not its description: the contribution of ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010

A further discipline on the transposition concerns representation. ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 differentiates an entity's architecture from an *architecture description* (AD) that expresses it. In the present application, the entity of interest is the legally constituted governance arrangement under investigation, whereas the regulation's authentic text is a primary source and the researcher's map, table or diagram is a description. Neither the text as document nor the diagram automatically constitutes the architectural whole inferred from the norm. This distinction is compatible with Section 4.3: a norm can have constitutive legal effects, whereas a scholarly diagram describing those effects does not.

The concepts of *viewpoint* and *view* suggest why no one graph should stand for the complete object. A position-and-authority view, a typed-relations view, a functional view, an evidence-and-legal-status view and a temporal or external-dependencies view can represent different aspects of the same arrangement. The standard's *correspondences* provide a useful representational analogue for keeping shared elements identifiable across those views. An architectural-description correspondence is, however, a link between representations and must not be mistaken for a legally constituted regulator–intermediary–target relation. Likewise, ISO stakeholders and their concerns orient the *purpose of a description*; they are not automatically identical with positions and objects legally specified by the GDPR.

This proposal draws selectively on the standard's descriptive vocabulary, as summarized in the supplied analytic study of Clauses 5–8; detailed normative requirements await primary-clause verification (`[CITATION CHECK]`). It makes no ISO-conformance claim, imposes no required modeling notation and does not import the standard as a theory of law or as an empirical validation procedure. Its value is the separation of ontology, evidence and plurality of views, which strengthens the disciplined transposition rather than replacing it.

## 6. A morphological-relational ontology of regulatory governance architecture

### 6.1 Definition

V6's definition is retained. Our specification is:

> A **regulatory governance architecture** is a *bounded*, *temporally indexed* configuration of legally constituted **positions** and **qualified relations** (and, where the legal source constitutes them, **institutional devices**), through which governance work, authority, discretion and accountability are distributed. Its properties arise from the **arrangement** of the relations and not from an inventory of its parts. It is reconstructed from **material inscriptions**, and its legal constitution, material instantiation and enacted operation are analytically distinct.

The specification adds boundedness and time-indexing (already required by §5.2.3), an explicit part/whole rule, and material anchoring with the separation of constitution, instantiation and operation. It takes nothing away.

### 6.2 Strata

```
 W  DOCUMENTARY CORPUS   a complete instrument (or set), fixed in version and time
 E  EVIDENCE            provisions · versions · institutional statements — the inscriptions
      │   each step is an inference governed by an explicit rule (V6 §6.7)
      ▼
 P  PARTS               Positions;  Institutional devices (a distinct type, not positions)
      │                 Normative assignments attached to positions (duties, powers)
      ▼
 R  QUALIFIED RELATIONS the reified relation: type · parties · modality · condition · validity
      │                 legal status · derivation · object · function · mechanism · instrument ·
      │                 authority · discretion · accountability pointer · dependencies
      ▼
 S  SUBCONFIGURATIONS   S1…Sn: local arrangements matched to a pattern rule; may overlap, nest
      │                 and connect provisions from different chapters
      ▼
 A  ARCHITECTURES       A1…Am: reconstructed by explicit criteria; not presumed one per instrument
      ▼
    multidimensional architectural PROFILE of the instrument

 M  EVIDENTIARY ANCHORING (transversal): which inscription, device or practice supports each claim
 D  DERIVED ANALYTIC DIMENSIONS (V6 Level III, outside the basic topology): strategies · governance logics
 X  ADJACENT EMPIRICAL LAYERS: implemented and operational arrangements · outcomes · sociomaterial enactment
```

**Evidence (E)** comprises provision-level inscriptions with their versions and provenance, and the institutional statements derived from them. **Parts (P)** are of two types. A *position* is a legally specified place in relations: an organization, an office, or an actor category; an office is not its holder. A position is a *type* (a generic legal category such as "national competent authority") or an *instance* (a specific institution or office), situated in a jurisdiction and a period; instances are recorded only with their own sources and are never inferred from a generic category, and a type carries the legal multiplicity the text states. An *institutional device* is a juridically constituted entity with its own boundary and rules, such as a register, an accreditation system or a reporting platform; a device is never an actor.

**Normative assignments.** Not every institutional statement establishes a relation between two positions. "Providers shall maintain appropriate records" assigns a duty, with an action and an object, to a position, without identifying a counterparty. Such *normative assignments* (duties, powers, permissions attached to a position) are kept as they are: they are intrinsic properties of positions, they enter the functional allocation and the distribution of authority, and they become relational only if some provision identifies a counterparty, forum or recipient. A counterparty is never invented to complete a binary representation. Objects, functions and mechanisms are carried by the assignment or the relation, according to the type of statement.

**Relations (R)** are legally constituted connections among positions, reified so that they can carry qualifiers and provenance. The pivotal importance V6 gives to relationships is preserved. Four things that are easily merged are held apart. *Modality* is the deontic character of the statement (obligation, permission, power, prohibition). *Condition* is what the norm makes its application depend on. *Legal status* is the status of the underlying norm in the legal order for the interval concerned (in force, not yet applicable, suspended, expired or repealed, or conditional on a further act); it is distinct from the epistemic status of the researcher's claim about the relation (§4.4). *Derivation* records whether the relation is stated directly or derived from several provisions. A prohibition is a norm about a relation or an action; it is not a connection that exists, and is recorded as a constraint on the positions concerned and not as a relation of the architecture. "Not found" is a statement about a search and not about the law.

**Subconfigurations (S)** are sets of relations that satisfy a pattern rule: a verification chain, a delegation triangle, an accountability loop. They are scoped to a function, a stage or a subsystem, may overlap or nest, and may connect provisions from different chapters of an instrument; the editorial structure of the text does not determine them. V6's named architectures (hierarchical, delegated, self-regulation, enforced self-regulation, meta-regulation, enhanced self-regulation, accreditation and assurance chains, networked, polycentric, multilevel; §5.2.2) are treated as patterns matched to subconfigurations, each with its own inference rule, and not as labels for whole laws, which is what V6's §5.2.3 asks for.

**The documentary whole (W) and the architectures (A).** W is the complete documentary corpus: instruments, jurisdiction, domain, interval and text version, and the rule that fixes membership. It is a boundary for evidence and not a presumption of unity. An *architecture* A_j is reconstructed from subconfigurations and relations by explicit inferential criteria, and declares its constituents, its criteria, its boundary within W and any external references it depends on. The number, boundaries and types of architectures are not fixed in advance; they need not be mutually exclusive; and V6's understanding that architectural dimensions and configurations may coexist is preserved. A property of "the architecture" is defined only relative to an A_j and its declared boundary. The output for an instrument is a *multidimensional profile*: properties attributed per S_i and A_j, each with a statement of scale and aggregation, together with a profile of epistemic statuses. It is not a single score or label, and it does not presuppose that the instrument constitutes one architecture.

**Derived dimensions (D).** Strategies and governance logics belong to V6's Level III. They are derived from configurations and are not coded at the first pass; they are not part of the basic topology of positions and relations, but they remain inside the conceptual frame. Strategies are annotations derived from sets of relations and, as V6 holds, are orthogonal to architecture; logics are an interpretive horizon above it. They are distinct from the **adjacent empirical layers (X)**, which concern how arrangements are implemented and operate.

### 6.3 Not every construct is a node

The twelve constructs of V6 do not become twelve kinds of vertex. Positions are the nodes of the actor-position projection. Objects, functions and mechanisms are carried by normative assignments and relations. Authority and discretion are attributes of relations that are also aggregated into distributions over the whole. Accountability is treated below. Strategies and logics are derived dimensions. Instruments are treated in two ways, one of which has a status. By default an instrument is a *typed characteristic* of an assignment or relation. Where the legal source gives it identity and an operator, rules or a bounded function, it is an identifiable *juridically constituted institutional device*, a distinct type of part that is neither a position nor an actor. Evidence that such a device has been *materially established* is not a third kind of entity: it is an evidenced establishment status of the constituted device, supported by evidence beyond the legal prescription. Positions, devices, documentary artefacts and institutions are not collapsed into one category of node. A graph is one analytic view in a possible architecture description and not the ontology or the legal object itself (cf. ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022): the actor-position graph shows positions as nodes and devices as interfaces on edges; other projections may show devices as nodes or link evidence to elements; each declares what it omits, and none is legal evidence.

### 6.4 A worked case: accountability

The treatment of accountability shows how the ontology operationalizes a contested construct. V6 lists accountability both as one of ten relationship types and as an attribute, and states that reporting alone does not constitute accountability (§4.3). The ontology keeps both roles linked by reference: one accountability relation exists as a single object, and other relations or arrangements carry a *pointer* to the accountability relations that qualify them, so that no observation is duplicated.

Two questions must be kept apart. The first is *substantive*: what kind of relation is it? The second is *evidential*: is the evidence sufficient to say so? Following the conception in which accountability is a relationship between an actor and a forum, in which the actor has an obligation to explain and justify its conduct (Bovens 2007; record and abstract verified, full text not yet consulted), we distinguish three substantive types:

- an **information relation**: reporting, disclosure or publicity without a duty of justification, never accountability on its own;
- an **answerability relation**: an identifiable actor–forum relation with a duty of explanation and justification and an institutionalized capacity of questioning, but without assessment/judgment capacity or a grounded possibility of consequences;
- an **accountability relation**: all of these, together with institutionalized assessment/judgment capacity and a possibility of consequences grounded in the legal source.

Evidential sufficiency is assessed separately and takes the values *sufficient* and *indeterminate*, the latter with the missing elements recorded. An assertion that a body "is accountable" with no forum, duty or channel identifiable in the corpus is not a fourth type of relation; it is a case of insufficient evidence for any type. Forums may be distributed or public when their relevant capacities are institutionally identifiable. Supervision alone does not establish accountability, though it may supply the forum's assessment capacity where a duty to explain is also present. That the capacities are legally constituted does not show that the arrangement operates; exercise is a separate observational claim.

### 6.5 Documents, artefacts and external references

A document plays two roles that must be distinguished. As an *evidentiary artefact*, it is an object of evidence with its own authenticity, provenance and date. As an *enacted instrument*, it carries the norms whose content constitutes positions and relations. The instrument is not itself a position, a relation or an actor, but its constitutive function is represented, through the legal-constitution status of what it constitutes and the provenance links to its provisions, and not by exclusion. Other documents can occupy roles in relations: a standard may specify what a verification requires; a certificate may be an output a relation requires; a register extract may evidence a claim. These roles are represented through the relation's object and instrument fields and through external references, while artefact metadata remains separate.

Evidence status belongs to the individual claim and not to the artefact. An evidence link records the claim, the artefact or artefacts, the level of evidence, whether the support is direct or indirect, and the limitation. The same artefact may support different claims at different levels: a register extract may support the claim that an entry was recorded on a date (a limited claim about the act of recording) and bear on the claim that the register exists, without supporting any claim about the entry's accuracy or effect. An artefact of unverified authenticity cannot raise the status of any claim above documentation.

Legally relevant positions, devices or standards are often constituted outside the immediate boundary of a corpus: in another instrument, in another part of the same instrument, or in a technical standard incorporated by reference. They are represented as typed *external references*, recording the referenced source and its kind (a legal instrument is distinguished from a technical standard or an administrative act, since source type is not legal force), the role of the reference, the verification status, whether it lies outside the boundary and is not expanded, and what element depends on it and how. A reference shows legal relevance only; it never demonstrates establishment or operation; no relations from the referenced source enter the architecture unless a boundary decision includes them. Unresolved references with a constitutive dependency also limit what may be asserted about absence.

Operational-only relationships, such as practice without a legal basis or practice departing from a constituted power, are not relations of the legally constituted architecture. They may be recorded in the adjacent empirical layer as observed arrangements, with an optional reference to the relation they supplement or depart from, so that any comparison between formal and operational architecture is explicit (V6 §6.13).

### 6.6 Properties of parts and of wholes

Four kinds of property are distinguished and not interchanged without a stated rule. *Intrinsic* properties are what the text confers on a position (a normative assignment, a function). *Relational* properties belong to a relation (type, direction, authority, discretion). *Positional* properties belong to a position *within* a configuration and depend on the whole: being a necessary stage, depth from the rule-making position, substitutability. *Emergent* properties belong to a subconfiguration or an architecture and arise from the arrangement of the relations. The atomistic and ecological fallacies are failures to respect these bearers.

V6's ten architectural dimensions can be assigned to bearers, but the assignment is not uniform across scales. Some are best stated first for each subconfiguration, function or stage (directness and intermediation, self-regulatory autonomy, relational depth, functional allocation) and then summarized for the whole. Others are properties of the whole as such (centralization, public-private allocation, vertical distribution, decision-centre plurality) or profiles over relations and positions (discretion distribution, accountability structure). The rule is: *scale and aggregation come before generalization.* A statement of a whole-level property declares its scale (which subconfigurations), its bearer and its aggregation rule; where subconfigurations differ, their values are reported before any summary. A statute that is strongly intermediated in monitoring and centralized in enforcement is not "an intermediated statute". The dimensions can be grouped in five families (composition and distribution; typed relational configuration; authority stratification and distribution; integration and segmentation; interface and boundary permeability); the grouping is a proposal for organizing comparison, not a validated measurement scheme. No dimension is reduced to a score: discretion is preserved by function, position and qualified relation, in keeping with V6's insistence that it is function-specific. Properties that depend on counts of instances (the number of decision centres) are computed only from evidenced instances or reported as bounded by the legal multiplicity the text states.

For an instrument examined as a documentary whole, the result of attributing properties in this way is not one architecture's profile but a profile across subconfigurations and architectures, with no number or type assumed in advance.

### 6.7 A controlled contrast: same composition, different connections

The aim of a contrast is to hold composition constant and vary only the connections. The following is a **hypothetical illustration, not legal evidence and not empirical validation.** Four positions occur in two imagined configurations: a regulatory target T, two intermediaries I1 and I2, and a regulator R. In both configurations there are the same four positions and the same total number of directed relationships (four), all of the same type (reporting), with the same modality (obligation), the same legal status (in force) and no stated conditions.

- *Configuration α (parallel).* T reports to I1; T reports to I2; I1 reports to R; I2 reports to R.
- *Configuration β (shared bottleneck).* T reports to I1; I1 reports to I2; I1 reports to R; I2 reports to R.

The inventory of positions, the number of relations and their type are identical. Per-position counts already differ (T has two outgoing reporting relations in α and one in β; I1 has one outgoing in α and two in β), so even a simple tally of relations by position carries some configurational information; the point is that the inventory and the type-and-number tallies cannot, by themselves, tell the configurations apart.

Describing the two as directed graphs, the routes from T to R are T–I1–R and T–I2–R in α, which share no intermediate position, and T–I1–R and T–I1–I2–R in β, both of which pass through I1. In the graph, I1 lies on every route from T to R in β (a shared bottleneck) and neither intermediary does in α; the longest route has two steps in α and three in β. These are positional and emergent properties of the arrangement: none is a property of any single relation ("T reports to I1" occurs in both), and none can be read from the tallies.

A limit is essential. Reachability in a graph is not the legal transmission of information. That I1 reports to R does not establish that what T reported to I1 reaches R, nor that R depends on I1 for knowledge about T. Whether I1 is a *legal* or *operative* bottleneck requires an additional rule and legal evidence, for example a provision requiring I1 to relay or incorporate T's report, or making R's decision depend on I1's report. Without such a rule the graph property is a *candidate positional property*, a question to put to the text, and not an architectural finding. The claim that no other route exists is also an absence-dependent claim, available only for a declared corpus whose coverage and unresolved external references have been stated. What the contrast shows is that questions about alternative routes, necessity and depth have different answers for the same composition, and that a configurational claim therefore depends on more than composition. It does not show that the configurational properties matter legally; that is for the rules and the evidence to establish.

## 7. Implications for relational leximetrics

### 7.1 The relation as the pivotal unit, and what is coded

V6's unit of observation links a provision to the object, the actors, the function, the mechanism and the relationship, supplemented by attributes (§6.3). The ontology preserves this and adds, for each observation, the status of the claims on which it rests. Coders need work only at the level of statements, assignments and relations; subconfigurations, architectures and their properties are *derived* by stated rules, which is what V6's ladder and its separation of direct coding from interpretive inference already require (§6.4). The commitments the ontology makes explicit (types of party, modality, condition, legal status, derivation from several provisions, external references) are the fields that distinguish a relation reconstructed from sources from one drawn from a diagram.

### 7.2 How the twelve constructs are specified

| V6 construct | Role in the ontology | Node? |
|---|---|---|
| Actors | Positions (type and instance); roles are relation-relative | Yes |
| Instruments | Typed characteristic of an assignment or relation; or an identifiable juridically constituted device, whose material establishment is a separately evidenced status | Device: not a position |
| Relationships | Reified qualified relations; the pivotal bridge | Reified |
| Objects, functions, mechanisms | Carried by normative assignments or relations; function, mechanism and instrument remain three separate slots | No |
| Authority, discretion | Relation attributes; distributions over the whole; no scalar aggregate | No |
| Accountability | A relation *and* a pointer qualifying another relation or arrangement; three substantive types and a separate evidential assessment | No |
| Strategies | Derived annotation; orthogonal to configuration | No |
| Architectures | Reconstructed from the documentary corpus, not identical to it; one instrument may yield several; named forms as patterns matched to subconfigurations | Whole(s) |
| Governance logics | Derived interpretive dimension above the architecture | No |

The specification does not alter V6's constructs; it says which kind of thing each is when an architecture is reconstructed. It also leaves V6's insistence intact that its framework is a conceptual map from which projects select the dimensions relevant to their domain. What the ontology contributes to that selection is a way of saying what depends on what, so that a project coding a subset can state which higher-order claims its evidence supports and which it does not.

### 7.3 A proposed bridge between general and specialised coding

Relational leximetrics is intended to be general; research on regulatory intermediation has developed specialised databases and codebooks organized around regulator, intermediary and target. We *propose*, for discussion, a mapping layer between provision-linked coded statements and reified relations: each statement would produce zero, one or several relations or assignments, with many-to-many links between provisions and relations, a flag for direct or derived, and a status. Such a layer would not replace a specialised codebook; the fields it records (actors, actions, recipients, functions) would supply the parties and functions of relations. It would make explicit what V6 calls the pivotal bridge (§6.3). No implementation exists; no such layer has been tested; and the proposal has not been discussed with the authors of any existing codebook.

A related point concerns a codebook variable of the kind that records the relationship between *different* architectures coded for one legal act (single, alternative, sequential, complementary, separate). On the ontology it is a relation among subconfigurations or architectures at the level of an act or corpus and not a property inside one architecture; it corresponds to V6's recognition that architecture may vary within a statute (§5.2.3). Its exhaustiveness and the exclusivity of its values cannot be assumed. (The variable is taken from an unpublished working codebook of the intermediation project; permission to cite is to be confirmed.)

### 7.4 Frequency, significance and the contrast design

V6 distinguishes frequency from architectural significance and proposes that significance depends on frequency, authority, centrality, exclusivity, sequence, consequence and substitutability, without generating one number (§6.6). The ontology gives this a home: composition (counts) is a descriptor of evidence and positions; significance is a claim about relations or subconfigurations that requires evidence on the other dimensions; and claims that depend on absence or necessity are subject to the closure rule. The contrast of §6.7 adds a test of whether an architectural claim depends on configuration or merely on composition.

### 7.5 Lessons from provision-level measurement

Seidl and Kosti (2026) decompose EU legislation into recitals and sub-article provisions and classify them by digital relevance, regulatory tone, regulated actor and rights, using a human-validated ensemble of language models distilled into smaller models, and report agreement between human coders and between humans and the models. They also state two limitations directly relevant here: provisions are treated as if of equal legal weight, and the way provisions relate to one another is not known (as read in the project copy of their unpublished manuscript; `[CITATION CHECK]` on any published version). Three lessons follow. Granularity is valuable and aggregates upward freely, which suits the nested units V6 proposes. Validation should be reported task by task, since agreement was lower for the more interpretive task (regulated actor) than for the least interpretive (digital relevance), consistent with V6's insistence that validation be examined at corresponding levels and not collapsed into one reliability statistic (§6.13), and with its distinction between direct coding and interpretive inference (§6.4). And granular classification alone does not reconstruct how provisions relate. The distinctive step on which the present proposal rests is the reconstruction of governance configurations from legally constituted relations rather than provision-level classification, which the authors identify as outstanding. We do not claim that provision-level work cannot develop in this direction; we claim that the ontology specifies what that development must preserve.

### 7.6 A complementary inferential criterion: V6's illustration

V6's stylized example (§6.15) has four provisions: periodic risk assessment by providers, verification by an accredited auditor, notification by auditors to a supervisory authority, and a power to suspend a provider's authorization. V6 concludes at the configurational level that the provisions establish third-party assurance combined with public supervisory enforcement, that "accreditation connects the verifier to a wider system of public control", and it carefully stops short of characterizing the whole statute as intermediated. The ontology offers a complementary criterion for the step involving accreditation. The claim that the verifier is connected to public control through accreditation requires a relation between an accrediting position and the verifier and a relation between a public authority and the accreditor; neither is supplied by the four provisions as stated, which establish verification, notification and suspension of the provider. With an explicit premise (a provision under which a public authority accredits or supervises the auditor) the stronger configurational claim is warranted and the pattern is an accreditation chain in the sense of §5.2.2; without it, the warranted claim is the narrower one. The example thereby serves its stated purpose: that the intermediate transformations must be explicit and recoverable. We offer this as an application of the ontology to the example and as a candidate criterion for future work, not as a correction.

### 7.7 A multi-view output without multiplying the legal object

The completed reconstruction may require more than a single visual representation. Drawing selectively on the description vocabulary of ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022, this article proposes several research views of the same provision-linked evidence: (i) positions and normatively assigned powers or duties; (ii) directed and legally qualified relationships; (iii) functional allocations and overlapping subconfigurations; (iv) legal status, provenance and claim-level warrant; and (v) interfaces, external references and temporal limitations. Each view should declare its question, inclusion rules, element identifiers and known omissions. Correspondences should preserve the identity of an entity or claim between views, without creating a new juridical relation. These are provisional research conventions inspired by the standard, not an ISO-conformant architecture description.

This also helps delimit the relation to Stratmann et al. (2026). Their architectural ecosystem visualizations establish a close methodological precedent for making regulatory interdependencies legible. The distinctive issue posed here is whether claims about a whole's properties can be traced through qualified relations and reconciled across different views without substituting the representational artifact for the legally constituted arrangement. The future GDPR demonstration will therefore report both the reconstructed relational claims and the limits of each analytical view.

## 8. Contribution, limitations and future empirical validation

### 8.1 Contribution

The article revisits its four propositions. **P1** is argued conceptually: configurations carry properties not identifiable from composition, and the contrast of §6.7 illustrates how this can be examined. **P2** is argued with a disciplinary justification that is still being strengthened: configurational theory supplies a treatment of positional and emergent properties, and the ontology assigns V6's dimensions to bearers. **P3** is developed with two contributions: the separation of documentary inscription, legal constitution, establishment and operation, and the observation that some enacted norms are constitutive of entities and relations while others only oblige, permit or condition, so that what is legally constituted, and therefore what is already "built" in the legal order, depends on the type of norm. **P4** is offered as a methodological possibility; no implementation or validation is claimed. The contributions are, concretely: a specification of V6's constructs by ontological role; a part/whole attribution rule with a contrast design; a substantive account of materiality as a constraint on reconstruction that is sensitive to the constitutive character of law; and operational criteria for contested constructs, with explicit treatment of incomplete evidence.

The article does not claim to have discovered that law constitutes governance architecture, to have shown that architectural properties are measurable, or to hold priority over existing research on regulatory intermediation, indirect governance or the architecture of governance regimes. One particularly close published antecedent has now been identified: Stratmann et al. (2026) already model and visualize EU regulation architecturally in their AI Act analysis. Its existence and central published claim have been checked against the publisher's abstract, while the detailed comparison here relies additionally on the supplied six-page fichamento; the full primary article has not yet been critically read. The proposed distinctiveness concerns the disciplinary part–whole grounding, separation of documentary and architectural boundaries, source-warranted attribution of properties, and the difference between architecture and description. The comparison with Abbott, Levi-Faur and Snidal (2017), Stratmann et al. and additional governance-architecture scholarship requires completion before making any claim of novelty. Nor does the article show that the ontology yields more discriminating architectural classifications than V6 already permits; that is an empirical question.

### 8.2 Limitations

- **No empirical validation.** The ontology has been checked only for internal consistency against invented cases, within an AI-assisted development process directed by the author, by the same process that formulated the rules, without blinding. The checks showed that rules were wanting and amended; they show nothing about adequacy on real legislation or about inter-coder reliability.
- **The transposition is conditional.** The incremental value of configurational theory rests on contrast designs and bearer assignment that remain to be instantiated in real legal text. If V6's dimensions capture them without remainder, the value is smaller than claimed.
- **Thresholds are unsettled.** The criteria for treating an instrument as a device and for the elements of accountability, including a *grounded* possibility of consequences, are judgments that real texts will test.
- **Philosophical pragmatism.** The pairing of critical realism with a limited use of sociomateriality is not a unified metatheory.
- **Reification and formalism.** Positions and relations "constituted by law" are not thereby real social relations. The separation of constitution, establishment and operation resists the inference, but a reader may still find the ontology too formalist; unwritten and customary constitution is, for now, a flagged exclusion.
- **Citation status.** Much of the theoretical apparatus, including the architectural genealogy of §2.2, is cited from bibliographic records and the author's understanding of the primary texts, not from verified readings; the literature on the materiality of law has not yet been engaged.
- **Systemic-architecture source limitation.** Stratmann et al. (2026) have been positioned using the published abstract and an analytical fichamento, pending close reading of the full article. For ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022, the abstract and scope are directly verified on the ISO site, while detailed uses of viewpoints, views and correspondences rest on the supplied fichamento pending checking against the standard's text. No claim of ISO conformity is made.
- **Relation to existing coding.** The mapping to specialised codebooks and the proposed bridge layer are proposals not yet discussed with the codebooks' authors.

### 8.3 What would count against the proposal

The proposal would be weakened if: no pair of real arrangements can be found with the same composition and different wiring bearing on an architectural property; positional claims cannot be warranted because corpora cannot be closed; coders cannot apply the criteria for devices and for accountability with acceptable agreement; the ten architectural dimensions turn out to exhaust what the ontology adds; or the constitutive-norm point (§4.3) turns out not to change any reconstruction.

### 8.4 A proposed empirical design and validation programme

The next task is empirical and is described here as a methodological proposal, not as a result. The proposed illustration will begin with an entire instrument, the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), taken as one predefined, dated legal instrument, and not with provisions chosen because they are expected to display a particular architectural type. The objective is to investigate which regulatory governance architectures and potentially overlapping subconfigurations can be reconstructed within the instrument when it is examined as a documentary whole, without selecting provisions because they are expected to demonstrate particular architectural types.

The design would include:

1. **The complete instrument,** with an explicitly fixed legal version and temporal reference, taken from the authentic published text and identified together with any official corrigenda, and not from a consolidated copy.
2. **A documented full-instrument coverage procedure,** stating how every operative provision is reached and recorded, so that coverage can be audited and corpus closure can be stated.
3. **Identification of normative assignments, positions, instruments and legally qualified relations,** with the norm types of §4.3 distinguished (an obligation to establish a body or device is not recorded as its establishment).
4. **Reconstruction of subconfigurations without predetermining architectural outcomes,** allowing them to overlap and to connect provisions from different chapters.
5. **Attribution of multidimensional architectural properties using explicit inference rules,** per subconfiguration and architecture, with scale and aggregation stated.
6. **Registration of relevant external legal references** (other instruments, national law, technical standards) without automatic expansion of the corpus.
7. **Explicit treatment of absence-dependent claims and of corpus-closure limitations.**
8. **A separate, subsequent stage of independent validation and human adjudication,** with blind coding of a subset, agreement reported by variable and by level of interpretation, and model-generated and human-coded observations distinguished.

For communication of this reconstruction, the study would produce coordinated but partial research views of the same legally grounded evidence: an inventory and normative-assignments view, a typed-relations view, overlapping-subconfiguration views, a multidimensional property profile, and an evidence/status/dependency view (§7.7). The views will retain shared element and claim identifiers, state their perspective and omissions, and be reconciled through representational correspondences. These are research conventions inspired by ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022, not a purported ISO-conformant architecture description, nor five separate legal architectures. The purpose is also to facilitate a future comparison with Stratmann et al. (2026), without assuming that visual elegance proves a legal-inferential claim.

Recitals may support interpretation but are differentiated from operative provisions in their juridical function: they are not generally treated as having independent binding force, a point of EU interpretive practice that must be verified against authoritative sources (`[CITATION CHECK]`). The design does not assume that the whole instrument constitutes only one architecture, and it does not claim that the instrument represents the complete European data-protection governance regime, which also involves other instruments, national law, case law and guidance. Further stages follow only afterwards: the contrast design applied to real instruments; sensitivity analysis to boundary and closure; competing classifications derived from the same relations, to test V6's expectation that alternative thresholds can be examined on the same data; and the extension to implementation, with sociomaterial approaches as a complementary lens. No extraction, machine coding or dataset development has been started.

### 8.5 Conclusion

Governance becomes an architecture, and not merely an arrangement, when it is a bounded configuration whose properties arise from the way legally constituted positions are connected, and when it can be recovered from identifiable sources by a procedure that can be repeated and challenged: the first condition concerns what the arrangement is, the second what a reconstruction requires. Levi-Faur's framework establishes that law builds such arrangements and offers the vocabulary and the rules of inference for reconstructing them. This article has argued that the framework gains in precision if configurational theory in architecture is used to say where properties reside, a realist account of reconstruction is used to say what may be claimed, and a substantive concept of materiality is used to say what carries the arrangement and what supports the claim. The legal case also teaches something to the architectural analogy it borrows. To the extent that norms directly establish entities and relations, what they constitute is constituted in the legal order before anything is built elsewhere; where they only oblige, permit or condition, nothing is yet constituted beyond the duty, the competence or the condition itself. The question of materiality is therefore not whether an architecture has been "constructed" but what has been constituted, in which medium, by which carriers, and on what evidence. And a documentary corpus is where reconstruction begins and not what it ends with: one instrument may yield no single architecture, or several. The contribution should be judged alongside existing legal architectural modeling, notably Stratmann et al. (2026), and must not mistake any selected diagram or view for its underlying object, a distinction reinforced by ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022. Whether the proposed ontological specification earns its place depends on verification of its theoretical sources and on the validation to which it is now to be put.
