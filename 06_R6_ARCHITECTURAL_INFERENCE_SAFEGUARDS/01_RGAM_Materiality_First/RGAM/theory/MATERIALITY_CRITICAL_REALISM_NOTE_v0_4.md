# Materiality and critical realism — compatibility note

> **Superseded in part by D03:** §2 (T-ont / T-epi) stands; the status table in §4 and the "provenance" framing are superseded by ontology §4 (four distinctions; five claim statuses; materiality as dependence on inscriptions and devices).

**Version:** v0_4 · **Date:** 2026-10-03 · **Status:** `WORKING_ANALYTICAL_NOTE`. All attributions to Bhaskar, Orlikowski, Leonardi, Hillier and others are `[CITATION CHECK]`; they are used here as positions to be verified, not as quotations.

## 1. The apparent tension

RGAM says an architecture claimed as *empirically reconstructed* needs a material referent (v0.3 §1). Critical realism says that what exists is not reducible to what is observed (the "epistemic fallacy": collapsing being into knowing). Read naively these collide: if existence were conditional on observation, materiality would become an observability test.

## 2. Resolution: two theses, kept apart

| Thesis | Content | Kind | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T-ont** | Legal-institutional architecture is not free-floating: it is constituted by socially real structures that depend on inscriptions, offices, registers, practices and infrastructures. "Material" is *not* "physical": digital inscriptions and legal texts have material carriers. | Ontological (weak) | Architecture is neither reduced to paper/hardware nor treated as purely ideal. |
| **T-epi** | A claim that *this* architecture has been **empirically reconstructed** must name the material referents and evidence through which it can be investigated, at the status claimed. | Epistemic / evidentiary | Governs **warrant** of the claim, not the existence of the thing. |

Critical realism is respected: an operational arrangement may exist and be undocumented (real but not yet empirical); T-epi only says that RGAM cannot *assert* it as reconstructed. Conversely a documented design may exist as an inscription without being instantiated — which is why *inscription ≠ implementation* (v0.4 §3).

Corollary (v0.3): without a defensible material referent, the correct statement is that we have an idea, ideal type or design — a **conceptually projected** architecture — not an empirical reconstruction.

## 3. What critical realism contributes (and what it does not)

Contributes (to be verified against primary texts):
- **Stratified ontology** (real / actual / empirical): a way to hold apart *structures and powers*, *events that occur* and *what is observed*.
- **Emergence**: wholes can have properties their parts lack — the philosophical backing for part/whole property separation.
- **Fallibilism with realism**: reconstructions are revisable without implying that architecture is "only a construct".

Does not contribute: any method for reading legal text, and no list of morphological properties. It is a frame, not a technique.

**Terminological hazard.** Bhaskar's "generative mechanism" is a different notion from V6's "mechanism" (a means of governing). RGAM uses *mechanism* only in the V6 sense; the realist notion, if invoked, is written "generative mechanism" in full.

## 4. Mapping the three materiality levels and four statuses to realist strata — loose, non-identical

| RGAM element | Closest realist reading | Warning |
|---|---|---|
| Formal architecture (inscribed in law) | A social structure whose existence depends on authoritative inscriptions | Do not equate with "the real" tout court: it is *one* real stratum |
| Operational architecture | Actual events/practices | Not deducible from the structure (functional/implementation fallacies, V6 §6.8) |
| What a reconstruction records | The empirical | Observation is evidence *about* both, never constitutive of them |

Statuses `conceptually_projected → normatively_inscribed → institutionally_instantiated → operationally_observed` are **claim statuses**, not strata. They need not form a linear life cycle (a platform may operate before a formal basis; a norm may lapse while a practice continues). A draft bill is not an enacted law; a mandated but unbuilt platform is not an operating platform.

## 5. Sociomateriality: compatibility problem and our scope decision

- Orlikowski's sociomateriality (2007; Orlikowski & Scott 2008) is generally read as a **relational ontology** in which social and material are constitutively entangled and entities have no inherent properties prior to enactment. That is *in tension* with a stratified realism that retains analytic separation of structure and practice. Leonardi's *imbrication* (2012) keeps the social and the material analytically distinct and is **closer** to realist analytic dualism. These positions are not one consensus; RGAM must not cite them as one.
- **Scope decision (v0.3/v0.4):** sociomateriality is **not** applied to the constitutive core (E/P/R/S/A). It is a complementary lens for layer X — enacted practice and transformation — and it is *not* reduced to "implementation" as a theory: the narrower use is a deliberate scope choice, not a claim about what sociomateriality means.
- **Therefore** RGAM does not need to settle the Bhaskar–Orlikowski dispute; it needs only to state that the two operate on different layers and that the combination is *pragmatic, not a unified metatheory*. This is a limitation to be declared, not hidden.

## 6. Objections to anticipate (carried to the ontology note)

1. *Is "materially anchored" just "provenance-tracked"?* If so it adds no ontology. Reply: it additionally (a) blocks promotion of a normative inscription to an institutional or operational claim, and (b) forces the three-way separation of inscription, instantiation, enactment. Residual risk: the term may still overpromise; consider "evidentially anchored" if David finds "material" misleading.
2. *Naïve physicalism / legal formalism.* Reply: T-ont is weak; institutional facts are not reducible to paper; the formal architecture is itself an inscription-dependent social structure.
3. *Reification.* Positions and relations "constituted by law" are not therefore real social relations; hence the formal/instantiated/operational separation.
4. *Legal materiality literature.* Work on legal materiality (e.g., Latour on the Conseil d'État; Pottage and Mundy on law and the material) is **unchecked**; do not claim the position is aligned with it until read.
