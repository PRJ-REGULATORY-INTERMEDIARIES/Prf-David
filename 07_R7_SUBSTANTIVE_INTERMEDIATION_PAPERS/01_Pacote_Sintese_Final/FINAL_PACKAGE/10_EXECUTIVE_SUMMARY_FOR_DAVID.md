# Executive summary
## Operationalizing relational leximetrics with AI — a four-case pilot

*Igor, October 2026. Preliminary; intended for discussion.*

---

**Why the work was done.** I set out to apply the inferential sequence in your paper — legal text → institutional statements → relationships → configurations → architecture → governance propositions — to four EU legal instruments, with AI assistance doing the coding: an operational test of whether machine-assisted coding can move from granular legal relations to architectural claims without silently adding assumptions the evidence does not support. It became much more elaborate than expected, and the reason is not about software.

**The central methodological lesson.** Machine-assisted coding is vulnerable in two distinct places, and only one is where I expected. Across four instruments and 264 paired comparative units, two isolated reconstructions produced **one** material disagreement at the level of evidence propositions — parsing what a provision says is nearly safe. But *relational* propositions — who, towards whom, in what direction, under what modality — produced **thirty**, at precisely the level your paper identifies as the pivotal bridge. And sixty-five of the ninety-six material disagreements were at the configuration, architecture and epistemic levels, where a language model will complete an architectural claim whether or not the evidence warrants one. The runs were isolated but not independent — same model — so their agreement is not evidence of correctness.

The characteristic upper-level failure is one your paper already names: the **container fallacy**, treating provisions in one act as one coherent architecture, which was the dominant disagreement in all four cases. It arrives as an artefact of task framing rather than carelessness — ask for "the architecture of this Regulation" and you receive one architecture per Regulation. A principle that makes a human coder hesitate does not make a model hesitate: for a machine executor, a principle with no output slot has no effect.

**The four-case result.** Regulation (EU) 2023/955, Regulation (EU) 2023/956, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and Council Directive 86/361/EEC were each reconstructed twice from one identical hash-verified official source: once applying your framework relatively directly, once with additional explicit controls over the inferential transitions. The comparison was preregistered and frozen before either set of outputs was opened, under a rule that longer output never converts a difference into a gain. It produced 56 blinded disagreement cards, each stating the common legal evidence and two unattributed propositions.

I examined six of the fifty-six under blinded A/B conditions, chosen for centrality, with identity revealed only afterwards. **Five supported the propositions produced under the additional controls; one supported the more compact baseline reconstruction.** Four of the five turned on restraint: declining a whole-act architecture for the Social Climate Fund and for CBAM; declining a whole-level property for the AI Act where the coupling condition failed; and declining an architectural claim for Directive 86/361, since a NET's constitution rests on a CEPT Memorandum of Understanding and "approved testing laboratory" status is fixed by the CEPT accreditation system, neither of which is in the Directive. The fifth surprised me: it named the Member State as seller of CBAM certificates, where the direct application left the seller unidentified although Article 20(1) says a Member State shall sell them — a failure of relational extraction, not of inferential restraint.

**The limitation.** Six of the 56 cards were assessed; the remaining fifty were not human-adjudicated and are not treated as adjudicated evidence. The six were purposively selected for methodological centrality, four of them at one comparative level, so the pattern is illustrative rather than representative and is no basis for any rate or performance claim. I made those six judgments myself, having designed both arms; blinding to arm identity does not remove that conflict, and independent adjudication is what would make the six count. The decisions are archived in the project record, but no written justification was captured at the time and none has been reconstructed — so each is auditable as a decision and not as reasoning. Further, both arms and the comparator ran on the same model, so the pilot cannot separate the effect of the controls from the effect of a longer, more structured prompt.

**The counterexample, which matters more.** On the AI Act, at the configuration level, the controlled reconstruction produced seven separate overlapping arrangements against three in the direct application — and the blinded decision supported the three. Nineteen subconfigurations did not answer the membership question; they deferred it, and the arm then resolved it at the architecture layer in the way the decision rejected. Greater granularity is not greater analytical quality. Restraint and decomposition are different virtues, and only the first was vindicated.

**What should be retained.** Eight safeguards, as fields on records that already exist rather than as new analytical strata: hashed source with legal status and reference date; named parties and modality per relation; a stated membership rule per configuration; one coupling question for any candidate whole, with "indeterminate" authorised; an external-constitutive-dependency flag; candidate status and stopping level as recorded values, with "no architecture warranted" available; one rival grouping per architectural claim, plus a coverage statement for absence-dependent properties; and uncertainty travelling with the claim. What should go is the apparatus around them — nineteen tables producing some 2,400 records against 96 material differences, improving traceability in not a single unit, and dropping your object and strategy/logic layers along the way.

**What should happen next.** Integrate the eight and retire the rest; obtain an independent adjudication of a subset of the 56; run a cheap arm-blind control to separate the controls from prompt structure. Then turn the simplified protocol on your substantive questions. One finding points the way: the second-order chain over intermediaries — accreditation, designation, reporting, investigation, suspension, withdrawal — is recoverable at relation level, and its *location* varies. The AI Act constitutes it internally, ten relations across Articles 28–38; CBAM's reconstruction finds only one inside the Regulation (Art. 18(2), accreditation on request), referring the rest out to Regulation 765/2008 and Implementing Regulation 2018/2067; Directive 86/361 refers almost all of it out to the CEPT accreditation system. I retrieved none of those external instruments, so these are claims about what each instrument refers out to. Whether a law constitutes or imports the governance of its own intermediaries looks to me like a variable worth a comparative design — a question for you rather than a conclusion of mine.
