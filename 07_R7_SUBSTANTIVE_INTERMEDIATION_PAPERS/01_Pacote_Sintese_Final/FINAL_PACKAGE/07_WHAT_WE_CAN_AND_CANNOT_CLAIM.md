# 07 — The claims boundary

**Purpose.** Fix what formal legal architecture, reconstructed from legal text, can and cannot support. Strictly.

**Source of the distinction.** David's manuscript, §6.13–§6.14: relational leximetrics reconstructs *formal legal architecture* and establishes the baseline against which *implementation architecture* and *operational architecture* can later be compared. Divergence between them is a research field, not measurement error. The four cross-level fallacies most relevant here are his: the **functional fallacy** (treating a formally assigned function as evidence it is performed) and the **implementation fallacy** (treating formal architecture as operational governance).

---

## Part 1 — Formal legal architecture can support claims about

### 1. Formal authority
Which actor holds a legally consequential capacity, and over what. *Example:* a notified body may issue, refuse, extend, restrict, suspend or withdraw a certificate for a high-risk AI system (Reg. 2024/1689, Arts. 44(2)–(3)). *Form of the claim:* "the law confers power P on actor A over object O."

### 2. Legal duties
Who must do what, towards whom, under what conditions. *Example:* an authorised CBAM declarant must ensure that declared total embedded emissions are verified by an accredited verifier (Reg. 2023/956, Art. 8(1)). Modality is codeable; compliance is not.

### 3. Discretion granted by law
Where the text confers judgment, a threshold, or a proportionality margin. *Example:* a notifying authority's choice to restrict, suspend or withdraw a designation "depending on the seriousness" of the failure (Reg. 2024/1689, Art. 36(4)–(5)). *Limit:* the claim is about the margin the law grants, never about how it is used.

### 4. Formal gateways
Where an actor's decision is a legal condition for an effect. *Example:* certification by an accredited verifier as a cumulative criterion for using actual emissions for imported electricity (Reg. 2023/956, Annex IV pt 5(e) with Art. 7(3)); recognition of a laboratory's certificate removing further tests for type approval (Dir. 86/361, Art. 6(2)). *Required qualification:* that it is a gateway *within the coded corpus*; whether an alternative route exists elsewhere is an absence-dependent claim needing a coverage statement.

### 5. Institutional dependence
Where one actor's status, competence or device is constituted by another instrument or actor. *Example:* "accredited verifier" status for CBAM purposes arises under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2018/2067; "approved testing laboratory" status under Directive 86/361 is fixed by the CEPT accreditation system. This is a claim about the legal source of a status, and it is one of the most reliable classes of claim the method produces.

### 6. Formal supervision
The existence, direction and content of second-order governance relations over an intermediary — accreditation, designation, reporting duties, investigation, restriction, suspension, withdrawal. *Example:* the ten-relation chain over notified bodies in Reg. 2024/1689, Arts. 28–38.

### 7. Formal accountability and answerability channels
Who must report, explain or submit to review, to whom, and on what trigger. *Example:* a notified body must inform the notifying authority of subcontracting and of cessation of activities (Arts. 33(1), 36(3)). *Limit:* the existence of the channel, not what passes through it.

### 8. Structural relationships and configurations
Which relations are legally linked, and — where the coupling is evidenced — which property attaches to the whole rather than to each part. *Required qualification:* this is the most demanding transition (§6.6) and the pilot's main finding is that it must be asserted explicitly and may legitimately come back indeterminate. In the four cases, 9 of 21 architecture candidates did.

### 9. The architecture's own boundary, and its indeterminacy
That an instrument does or does not constitute a single bounded architecture — including the negative result. The Directive 86/361 reconstruction stopped below architecture; that is a claim the method can support, and it is as much a result as a positive one.

### 10. Comparative structural difference
That two instruments allocate the same function differently — e.g. that the AI Act constitutes the supervision of its intermediaries internally while CBAM imports it. *Limit:* difference in formal design, nothing more.

## Part 2 — Formal legal architecture alone cannot establish

### 1. Actual effectiveness
Whether the arrangement achieves its objective. Nothing in the text bears on it.

### 2. Real expertise gains
Whether routing verification to a third party improves the quality of verification. The law can require competence and independence; whether competence exists is an empirical question about organisations.

### 3. Capture
Whether an intermediary serves the interests of those it assesses. Formal independence requirements are evidence that the legislature anticipated the risk, not evidence about the outcome. The AI Act's provision allowing a provider to choose any notified body (Art. 43(1)) is equally consistent with healthy competition and with competitive laxity; the text cannot distinguish them.

### 4. Implementation quality
Whether designations are made, audits conducted, certificates refused, deadlines met. David's implementation fallacy (§6.8).

### 5. Behavioural consequences
How regulated actors respond; whether obligations change conduct; whether gateways deter.

### 6. Practical accountability
Whether an answerability channel produces answers. A reporting duty establishes a channel; it does not establish that information flows or affects decisions (§6.8).

### 7. Real-world performance
Costs, delays, market concentration among intermediaries, enforcement rates, outcomes.

### 8. Operational centrality
Whether a formally indispensable intermediary is operationally pivotal, or a formally peripheral one has become indispensable in practice. §6.14 is explicit that private standards may acquire significance beyond the formal design and that delegated authority may remain unused.

### 9. Regime boundaries
Where the wider regulatory regime begins and ends. The CBAM baseline reconstruction illustrates the discipline: Art. 1 states that CBAM complements the EU ETS, and the reconstruction still declined a regime claim because the corpus does not supply the material to map the boundary.

### 10. Anything resting on an unverified external instrument
In this pilot, 148 external references, 73 constitutive, none retrieved. Any claim whose legal constitution depends on them is undetermined or restricted to the declared corpus. This includes the central second-order question for CBAM and for Directive 86/361.

### 11. Anything resting on absence
Necessity, exclusivity, substitutability, "no alternative path," "the instrument contains no such relation." These require a declared coverage statement. Without one, "not found" is a statement about the search.

## Part 3 — Three claims specific to this exercise that must not be overstated

**On the comparison.** It establishes *where* the two procedures diverge and nothing about which is more accurate. Fifty of the 56 cards were not human-adjudicated and are not treated as adjudicated evidence.

**On the six assessed cards.** They are a purposive set of six from fifty-six, four of them at one comparative level, decided by the study’s own designer and archived without recorded justifications. They support "here is a pattern worth testing." Because they were purposively selected for methodological centrality, the pattern is illustrative rather than representative: it does not support a rate, a proportion, an accuracy, a win, or a claim of superiority. The one card that went the other way (HQ-AI-12) is part of the evidence, not an exception to it.

**On attribution.** Both arms and the comparator ran on the same model, with reasoning effort not exposed to the executor. The pilot cannot separate the effect of the controls from the effect of a longer, more structured prompt. No claim here should be read as establishing that the controls, as such, caused the difference.
