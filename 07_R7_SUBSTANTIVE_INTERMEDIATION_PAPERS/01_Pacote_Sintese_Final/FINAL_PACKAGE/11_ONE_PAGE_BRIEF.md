# From coding to architecture — one page

*Igor, October 2026. Preliminary.*

I applied your inference ladder to four EU instruments with AI doing the coding. Parsing what a provision *says* turned out to be nearly safe. Recording *who is related to whom* was not. Inferring upward from there is where most of the trouble lives.

**What happened.** The Social Climate Fund Regulation, CBAM, the AI Act and Council Directive 86/361/EEC were each reconstructed twice from one identical, hash-verified official text — once following your framework directly, once following the same framework but having to declare every inferential step: what couples these configurations, what property attaches to the whole, what rests on instruments outside the text, where to stop. The runs were isolated but not independent (same model), so their agreement is not evidence of correctness. Comparison rules were frozen in advance, including the one that mattered: more output never counts as a better result.

**The finding.**

```text
LEGAL TEXT
   |  decomposition into statements .....  1
   v
INSTITUTIONAL STATEMENTS
   |  parties, direction, modality ...... 30
   v
RELATIONSHIPS
   |  membership and boundary ........... 26
   v
CONFIGURATIONS
   |  coupling; property of the whole ... 23
   v

   |  stopping level; uncertainty ....... 16
   v
GOVERNANCE PROPOSITIONS

96 material disagreements between the two runs, by the level of the
proposition at issue, mapped onto the transition that produces it.
```

Thirty at the relational level surprised me, since that is the level your paper calls the pivotal bridge. The clearest instance: CBAM Article 20(1) says that "A Member State shall sell CBAM certificates…", and the direct run recorded the certificate arrangement with the seller unidentified.

Above that, one disagreement recurred in all four cases: whether the instrument constitutes **one** architecture. This is your container fallacy, and here it was the default. Ask for "the architecture of this Regulation" and you get one architecture per Regulation. A model does not hesitate as a human coder does; it writes fluent architectural prose from thin evidence as readily as from thick.

**The evidence and its limits.** The comparison produced 56 blinded cards, each with two unattributed propositions over the same legal evidence. I decided six blind, chosen for centrality, learning which was which afterwards. Five supported the more careful run: the CBAM seller case; no whole-act architecture for the Fund or for CBAM; no whole-instrument property for the AI Act where the coupling failed; and no architectural claim at all for Directive 86/361, since "approved testing laboratory" status is fixed by the CEPT accreditation system and a NET rests on a CEPT memorandum, neither of which is in the Directive. The other fifty were not human-adjudicated and are not treated as adjudicated evidence. Six purposively selected decisions are a pattern worth testing, not a rate or a result — and I made them myself, having designed both runs, recording no reasons at the time. That is the weakest point here and the easiest to fix.

**The sixth went the other way, and it is the most useful.** On the AI Act the careful run split the high-risk and market-surveillance relations into seven arrangements; the direct run used three; I chose three. Nineteen subconfigurations had not answered the membership question — they deferred it, and the run then resolved it at the architecture level in the way I rejected. Restraining an upward inference and subdividing a representation are different operations, and only the first was vindicated.

**What I would keep.** Eight fields folded into the existing protocol: hashed source with status and date; named parties and modality per relation; a membership rule per configuration; one coupling question whose honest answer may be "indeterminate"; a flag where an actor or specification is constituted outside the text; a recorded stopping level with "no architecture warranted" available; one rival grouping per architectural claim; uncertainty attached to the claim. The rest — nineteen tables, some 2,400 records against 96 real disagreements, no traceability gain in any case, and your object and strategy layers dropped along the way — should go.

**Why it may be worth your time.** One safeguard produced a finding about the law, not the method. The chain governing intermediaries — accreditation, designation, reporting, investigation, suspension, withdrawal — is recoverable from text, and its *location* varies. The AI Act constitutes it internally: ten relations, Arts. 28–38. CBAM refers most of it out, the only such relation found inside the Regulation being accreditation on request (Art. 18(2)); Directive 86/361 refers almost all of it out. I retrieved none of those external instruments, so this is a claim about what each instrument refers out to. Whether a law constitutes or imports the governance of its own intermediaries may be a structural variable in its own right — measurable from text, invisible without that flag, and directly on your question of who governs the governors. Four instruments cannot establish it; they can show it is measurable. My question is whether that is worth a comparative design.
