# V8 native analysis — CBAM

## Scope and inferential stance

This is a reconstruction of formal legal design in Regulation (EU) 2023/956 as represented by the frozen English XHTML. It does not establish implementation, actual practice, effectiveness, the contents of referenced instruments, or the complete regulatory regime. The evidence-to-inference chain is kept visible in `V8_CLAIM_REGISTER.csv`; typed relations and their attributes are in `V8_RELATION_REGISTER.csv`.

## Level I: governance elements

The principal governed objects are listed imported goods, their embedded emissions, emissions information and verification records, and the CBAM certificates that must be acquired, held and surrendered (Articles 1–3, 6–9, 20–24). The scheme also governs access to importation through declarant authorization (Articles 4–5, 17, 25). Article 1 expressly situates CBAM as complementary to the EU ETS and as a replacement for specified free-allocation mechanisms in addressing carbon leakage; this is a formal linkage, not proof that the wider EU ETS regime is exhausted by this text (C01).

The legal actor categories include the Commission; Member State competent authorities and customs authorities; importers and authorized CBAM declarants; indirect customs representatives; third-country operators and installations; accredited verifiers and national accreditation bodies; the CBAM Committee; the European Parliament and Council; and persons or organizations eligible to notify suspected circumvention (Articles 2–35). Roles are situated and can change by function: for example, a third-country operator may request registry entry and calculate emissions, while the authorized declarant remains responsible for the annual declaration and surrender (Articles 6, 10; C04, C06).

Core governance functions are authorization, emissions calculation and verification, reporting, registry administration, certificate sale and surrender, risk analysis, review, correction, enforcement, circumvention investigation, coordination, and periodic evaluation (Articles 5–30, 32–35). Mechanisms include conditional market access, self-reporting, third-party verification, risk-based review, information exchange, certificate surrender and penalties. Instruments include the authorization status, annual CBAM declaration, CBAM registry/account, verification report, common central platform, certificates, review/correction procedure and penalty. These terms are not interchangeable: for example, verification is a function/mechanism carried through accredited-verifier arrangements, while the report is an evidentiary instrument (Articles 6–8, 14, 18, 20, 26; C03, C05, C08).

Several legal assignments do not themselves establish a specific counterpart relationship. Article 11 assigns Member States the designation of competent authorities; Article 12 assigns coordination/support functions to the Commission; Article 19(1) gives the Commission an oversight role; Article 30 assigns reporting/evaluation to the Commission. These are preserved as assignments (C16; C14). Where the text separately identifies an addressee or information flow, that particular connection is coded as a typed relation.

## Level II: authority, discretion and accountability

Authority is distributed by function. The Commission establishes and operates the registry (Article 14), reviews declarations and performs Union-level risk analysis (Articles 15 and 19), while competent authorities grant declarant status, review/correct declarations and impose penalties (Articles 17, 19, 26, 35). Customs authorities control import admissibility and transmit customs information (Articles 25 and 33). The text also delegates specified technical rulemaking to the Commission under implementing or delegated acts, subject to differentiated procedures and institutional controls (Articles 2, 7–9, 18, 20–21, 25, 27–31). These are function-specific grants, not a single undifferentiated authority relation (C07, C09, C10).

Discretion is bounded but not absent. Article 17 ties authorization to enumerated criteria and a consultation process; Articles 19 and 15 provide risk-based review/control choices; Article 26(2) specifies a penalty range and relevant factors; Article 27 gives the Commission an evidence-based investigation pathway. The source supports identifying the range and conditions of formal choice, but not how officials exercise that choice in practice (C10, C11).

Accountability is most clearly evidenced where duties, information, review and consequences connect. Declarants must keep records capable of supporting verifier checks and administrative review (Article 7(5)–(6)); competent authorities communicate review outcomes and impose penalties following notice of reasons and amount (Articles 19, 26); the Commission reports to the Parliament and Council (Article 30); and delegated acts are subject to objection/revocation by Parliament or Council (Article 28). Reporting alone is not treated as a full accountability relationship unless the text also supports an answerability, assessment or consequence link. The analysis does not infer general democratic accountability or effective sanctioning from these clauses (C12–C14).

## Level III: configurations and architecture

Five configurations are supported and registered: (1) authorization and controlled import access; (2) emissions measurement, verification and declarant reporting; (3) registry-mediated customs/administrative information and oversight; (4) certificate pricing, sale, holding, surrender and consequences; and (5) technical rulemaking, coordination and review. Each configuration combines relations and instruments around a governance function; it is not a frequency cluster. The cross-article linkages matter: for example, authorization gates importation, registry accounts carry certificate data, and annual surrender obligations are checked against verified emissions (Articles 4–8, 14–26; C02–C10).

At the highest warranted level, these configurations support an explicit inference of a **formal CBAM governance architecture**: the Regulation organizes a linked authorization, measurement/verification, registry, certificate and enforcement structure across Union and Member State public bodies, regulated declarants, third-country operators, and accredited private verifiers. This is a claim about the configuration legally constituted by this Regulation, not a claim that any one actor or provision is the architecture, nor that the architecture operates as designed (C15). The architecture is not reduced to a categorical “hybrid” or “centralized” type: central Commission functions coexist with national authorization/enforcement, customs controls, and verifier roles, and their practical relative weight is not observable here.

No claim is made that the Regulation alone establishes a complete regulatory regime. Article 1's express complementarity with the EU ETS supports a formal connection to another legal scheme, but regime boundaries and membership require evidence beyond this source. No governance logic or comparative strategy label is assigned: the legal design contains several forms of coordination and control, but one source and no implementation evidence do not resolve a dominant or coherent logic. The architecture and stopping decision are recorded in `V8_ARCHITECTURE_REGISTER.md` and `V8_STOPPING_RULE_RECORD.md`.
