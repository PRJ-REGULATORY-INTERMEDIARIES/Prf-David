# V8 native analysis — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

## Scope and reading rule

This is a formal legal-design reconstruction of the complete English text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It does not establish implementation, compliance, practical performance, or the boundaries of the wider AI regulatory regime. Claim IDs below link each inference to legal anchors and direct evidence in the claim register. “Explicit” identifies what the Regulation assigns or states; “derived” marks relational coding from explicit provisions; “higher-order” marks configurational inference. The object of an architectural claim is the Regulation's formal design, not an empirical system in operation.

## Level I — elements and typed relationships

The governed objects are differentiated by legal status and use: AI systems and general-purpose AI models; high-risk and other systems; certain AI practices; system outputs and data; and, in relevant provisions, risks to health, safety, fundamental rights and other protected interests (C01; Articles 1–3, 5–7, 9–15, 51–55, 79). The Regulation's scope reaches specified providers, deployers, importers, distributors, product manufacturers, authorised representatives and affected persons, while Article 2 supplies material and personal scope limits (C02; Articles 2–3).

For high-risk systems, the text allocates substantial governance work to providers: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency design, human-oversight design, quality management, conformity assessment, corrective action and post-market monitoring (C03; Articles 9–19, 20, 43, 72). Deployers receive distinct use-side duties, including use in accordance with instructions and assignment of competent human oversight (C04; Article 26). These are normative assignments, not evidence that the actors perform them in practice.

The Regulation establishes conformity-assessment bodies and notified bodies as third-party assessors within a legally specified route. Member State notifying authorities assess/designate/notify and monitor those bodies; the Commission and other Member States receive notifications. In specified Article 43 circumstances, assessment may be internal or may involve a notified body, so third-party assessment is not a universal route for every high-risk system (C05; Articles 28–34, 43). The instrument set includes risk-management systems, quality-management systems, technical documentation, logs, standards/common specifications, conformity-assessment procedures, declarations of conformity, CE marking, registration, post-market monitoring, complaints and enforcement measures (C03–C08; Articles 9–19, 40–43, 47–49, 71–85, 99–101).

The market-surveillance and corrective-action relationships are textually structured. Providers must furnish information on reasoned request and report relevant incidents; Member State market-surveillance authorities evaluate systems, require corrective action and can use specified restriction/prohibition measures. Article 81 gives the Commission a Union safeguard role where objections arise or a national measure appears contrary to Union law (C06; Articles 20–21, 73–85). Article 77 separately gives designated fundamental-rights authorities access to specified documentation within their mandates, with notification to the market-surveillance authority; this is an access and coordination link, not a generic supervisory hierarchy (C07; Article 77).

General-purpose AI models with systemic risk follow a distinct formal pathway. The Commission has exclusive supervisory and enforcement powers for Chapter V, entrusting implementation to the AI Office; the Regulation identifies the AI Office with a Commission function. The Commission/AI Office may request information and conduct evaluations, with consultation of the Board for Article 92 evaluations; a scientific-panel alert can trigger consideration. The Commission may request mitigation, restriction, withdrawal or recall, and may make offered commitments binding by decision (C08; Articles 3(47), 88, 90–94). This pathway is not collapsed into the Member State market-surveillance route.

## Level II — authority, discretion and accountability

Authority is distributed by function and object. Member States designate notifying and market-surveillance authorities; notifying authorities control the entry and monitoring of conformity-assessment bodies (C09; Article 28). Market-surveillance authorities possess evaluation and corrective powers under Articles 74 and 79–84, subject to procedures and Union safeguard review (C06, C10). The Commission has specified coordinating, safeguard and implementing roles and exclusive Chapter V supervision/enforcement for GPAI models (C10; Articles 81, 88, 91–98). These statutory capacities should not be generalized beyond their assigned subject matter.

Discretion is bounded and function-specific. Provider choices exist among conformity-assessment routes only under Article 43's conditions; deployers retain freedom to organize resources and activities for oversight implementation subject to the duties in Article 26(1)–(2); and authorities make contextual evaluations and select proportionate corrective/enforcement measures within prescribed criteria and procedural safeguards (C11; Articles 26, 43, 79–84, 99–101). C11 does not infer free-standing discretion from actor titles.

Accountability provisions are heterogeneous. Provider records, declarations, requests for information, incident reports and annual monitoring feed evidence to authorities (Articles 11–12, 20–21, 47, 72–73). These information channels support oversight but are not by themselves a complete accountability relation. More structured answerability and review appear in procedural protections: hearing and file-access rights before EDPS fines, review of Commission fines by the Court of Justice, and Article 99(10)'s procedural safeguards and judicial remedies (C12; Articles 94, 99–101). The Regulation also provides Commission reporting and notification links, but reporting alone is not coded as accountability absent assessment or consequence (Articles 28, 30, 99–101).

## Level III — strategy and configurations

Three configurations are supported. First, high-risk-system governance combines provider-side lifecycle controls and documentation with deployer-side use/oversight duties and conditional external conformity assessment (K01; C03–C05, C11). Its mechanism mix includes organizational self-control, information/traceability, technical requirements and third-party assessment where the legal route requires or permits it. The configuration is not reducible to “self-regulation” or “third-party regulation”: legal responsibility remains assigned to operators while public designation and surveillance structures constrain the arrangement.

Second, the market-surveillance configuration connects national evaluation and corrective authority, operator information/cooperation duties, access by designated fundamental-rights bodies, and a Commission safeguard mechanism (K02; C06, C07, C09–C12). It is multilevel in legal allocation and includes both national action and Union-level review/coordination. The legal text supports this structural description; it does not show how frequently these powers are used or which level dominates in practice.

Third, Chapter V creates a Commission-centered GPAI supervision/enforcement configuration with AI Office implementation, information/evaluation powers, scientific-panel alerts, Board consultation/information, and provider-facing measures subject to procedural rights (K03; C08, C10, C12). This is a distinct subject-matter pathway within the Regulation; it does not establish that the Commission controls every AI Act function.

## Bounded architectural inference

Taken together, K01–K03 support one higher-order claim: the Regulation constitutes a differentiated, hybrid, multilevel formal governance architecture. “Differentiated” refers to distinct governance pathways for high-risk systems and GPAI models; “hybrid” refers to the combination of operator-internal duties, external conformity assessment in defined routes, and public authority; “multilevel” refers to the legally linked national and Union roles in relevant pathways. The bearer is the formal legal design constituted by this Regulation (C13; K01–K03). This is configurational significance: different actors receive linked governing functions, authority and constraints at lifecycle stages and for distinct regulatory objects. It is not a frequency inference or a claim that one actor dominates the entire instrument.

The evidence does not warrant a single unqualified governance logic for all provisions. Hierarchical enforcement is visible in specified public powers, while operator management duties and qualified third-party assessment shape other functions. No claim is made that the Act forms or exhausts a regulatory regime: regime boundaries require evidence beyond this statute and are not inferred from its container (U05). No operational or implementation architecture is inferred. Analysis stops at the formal architecture level for this bounded object.
