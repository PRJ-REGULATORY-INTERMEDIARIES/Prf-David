### [¶4] Codebook

#### [¶5] Introduction

[¶6] This codebook documents the variables, coding categories, and coding rules used in the Regulatory Intermediation in European Digital Governance dataset. It explains the analytical purpose of the coding scheme and how these purposes are operationalized through the individual variables and their possible values.

[¶7] The coding has three principal purposes:

[¶8] Historical and institutional mapping: to identify when, where, and in which regulatory instruments regulatory intermediaries emerge. Variables describing the regulatory instrument, including its year, sector, regulatory scope, and instrument type, provide the basis for this mapping.

[¶9] Governance analysis: to identify how regulatory authority and functions are allocated among European Union institutions, Member States, designated public authorities, regulated organizations, and regulatory intermediaries. This includes identifying who regulates, how self-regulation is operationalized, who the regulatory intermediaries are, and what regulatory functions they perform.

[¶10] Analysis of institutional variation: having identified the regulatory intermediaries and their functions, the third purpose examines how those intermediaries are themselves organized and regulated. This includes their function, organizational basis, the pathways through which they are recognized, the intensity of the obligations imposed on them, and how they are supervised.

[¶11] These purposes define what information is collected and how it is translated into coding variables. Comparisons across periods, sectors, regulatory instruments, and regulatory mechanisms constitute subsequent uses of the coded data rather than separate coding purposes.

[¶12] For each variable, the codebook provides its definition and possible values. For variables requiring interpretive judgment, it also provides coding or decision rules to clarify how to apply the categories.

#### [¶13] General Coding Conventions

[¶14] Unit of analysis: A European Union legislative instrument or policy document coded in the dataset.

[¶15] Intermediary observation: Each regulatory intermediary identified within an instrument is coded as a separate observation. An instrument that establishes or recognizes multiple intermediaries therefore appears in multiple rows, with one row for each intermediary.

[¶16] Substantive intermediary reference: Code an actor or organizational arrangement as a regulatory intermediary when the instrument substantively assigns it a regulatory function within or between the regulator, regulatory target, and beneficiaries. Technical cross-references alone are not coded as intermediary observations.

[¶17] Relevant provisions: Coding is based on the relevant provisions of the regulatory instrument, including Chapters, Articles, Recitals, Annexes, and Appendices. Where several provisions define the same intermediary's role, record all relevant provisions.

[¶18] Multiple functions: Code regulatory functions separately; they are not mutually exclusive. An intermediary may therefore perform more than one regulatory function.

[¶19] Use the following values consistently:

[¶20] Yes: The regulatory instrument provides sufficient evidence that the intermediary performs the specified regulatory function.

[¶21] No: A regulatory intermediary is identified, but the instrument does not assign the specified regulatory function to that intermediary.

[¶22] N/A: No regulatory intermediary is identified in the instrument, or the category does not apply to the intermediary or instrument being coded.

[¶23] Unclear: Used where the available regulatory instrument does not permit a reliable classification.

[¶24] Unclear/cross-reference: Used where the relevant characteristic cannot be reliably classified from the instrument being coded because its determination depends on a cross-reference to another legal source.

### [¶62] 2. Governance analysis

[¶63] These variables identify how regulatory authority and functions are allocated among European Union institutions, Member States, designated public authorities, regulated organizations, and regulatory intermediaries. The analysis addresses four questions: who regulates, how self-regulation is operationalized, who the regulatory intermediaries are, and what functions they perform.

#### [¶64] Who regulates?

[¶65] Definition: Identifies and describes how regulatory authority is allocated under the regulatory instrument, including the roles of European Union institutions, Member States, and designated public authorities. The variable distinguishes general references to Member States from specifically identified regulatory authorities, such as competent authorities, national regulatory authorities, supervisory authorities, and the European Commission, and records how regulatory responsibilities are distributed among them.

[¶66] Values: Free text.

#### [¶67] How is self-regulation operationalized?

[¶68] Definition: Identifies and describes how the regulatory instrument combines legally prescribed requirements with reliance on regulated organizations or other non-state actors in implementing regulatory requirements or achieving regulatory objectives. The variable records how self-regulation is incorporated into the regulatory arrangement, including the responsibilities and discretion retained by regulated organizations and the use of process-based, performance-based, principle-based, or other forms of regulation that rely on regulated entities or third parties for implementation.

[¶69] Values: Free text.

#### [¶70] Who are the regulatory intermediaries?

[¶71] Definition: Identifies and describes the actors or organizational arrangements that perform regulatory functions within or between the regulator, regulatory target, and beneficiaries. The variable records the intermediary or intermediaries established, recognized, or otherwise relied upon by the regulatory instrument and their position within the regulatory arrangement. Technical cross-references that do not substantively assign a regulatory function are not treated as intermediary references.

[¶72] Values: Free text.

#### [¶73] What functions do they perform?

[¶74] Definition: Identifies and describes the regulatory role performed by the intermediary under the regulatory instrument, including the tasks, responsibilities, and authority assigned to it and its relationship to regulators and regulated organizations. The free-text description records the intermediary's substantive role in the regulatory arrangement before translating that role into the standardized functional categories used to analyze institutional variation.

[¶75] Values: Free text.

[¶76] Relevant provision(s)

[¶77] Definition: Identifies the provision or provisions of the regulatory instrument in which the regulatory intermediary and its regulatory role are established, recognized, or specified.

[¶78] Values: Free text.

[¶79] Coding rule: Record the relevant Chapter, Article, Recital, Annex, or Appendix. Where several provisions define the same intermediary or its regulatory role, list all relevant provisions.

### [¶80] 3. Analysis of institutional variation

[¶81] After identifying the regulatory intermediaries and describing their functions in the governance analysis, these variables examine how regulatory intermediaries are organized and regulated. They translate the descriptive coding of intermediaries into standardized variables that enable comparison of their functions, organizational basis, recognition pathway, obligation intensity, and supervision across regulatory instruments.

#### [¶82] Regulatory functions

[¶83] Definition: Identifies the regulatory functions assigned to the intermediary. Functions are coded separately and are not mutually exclusive; an intermediary may therefore perform multiple functions.

#### [¶84] Rule-setting / Code creation

[¶85] Definition: Participation in developing or designing codes, rules, or standards.

[¶86] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶87] Assurance

[¶88] Definition: Assessing or verifying whether a product, system, organization, or activity complies with regulatory requirements.

[¶89] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶90] Monitoring

[¶91] Definition: Tracking conduct, risks, or compliance and, where applicable, transmitting information to an oversight body.

[¶92] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶93] Internal compliance

[¶94] Definition: Assisting with the implementation of regulatory requirements or internal controls within a regulated organization.

[¶95] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶96] Dispute redress

[¶97] Definition: Resolving disputes or providing redress outside ordinary judicial proceedings.

[¶98] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶99] Complaint handling

[¶100] Definition: Receiving, assessing, or handling complaints.

[¶101] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶102] Auditing

[¶103] Definition: Conducting a systematic and, where applicable, periodic examination of activities, processes, or compliance.

[¶104] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶105] Insurance

[¶106] Definition: Providing or administering insurance relevant to regulatory compliance or the regulated activity.

[¶107] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

#### [¶108] Enforcement

[¶109] Definition: Taking action, as an intermediary rather than as a regulatory authority, to secure compliance, remedy violations, or support the application of regulatory requirements.

[¶110] Values: Yes / No / N/A.

[¶111] Coding rule: Code each function independently based on the intermediary's role under the regulatory instrument. The general Yes/ No /No/N/A conventions above apply.

#### [¶112] Organizational basis

[¶113] Definition: Identifies the organizational basis of the regulatory intermediary, distinguishing the principal institutional setting in which it operates.

[¶114] Values:

[¶115] Market/private-capable: Private or commercially organized actors capable of providing the intermediary function through market-based arrangements.

[¶116] Epistemic/professional: Actors whose intermediary role rests principally on professional, technical, scientific, or other specialized expertise.

[¶117] Associational: Industry, professional, or other membership-based associations performing intermediary functions.

[¶118] Public/quasi-public: Public bodies or organizational arrangements closely connected to public authority while performing an intermediary role.

[¶119] Unclear: The available regulatory instrument does not permit reliable classification.

[¶120] N/A: No regulatory intermediary is identified, or the variable does not apply.

#### [¶121] Recognition pathway

[¶122] Definition: Identifies the institutional mechanism through which the regulatory intermediary acquires the status or authority necessary to perform its regulatory role.

[¶123] Values:

[¶124] Designation/notification: The intermediary is formally designated or notified under the regulatory framework.

[¶125] Vetting/accreditation: The intermediary is subject to an assessment of its competence, qualifications, independence, or other relevant criteria before recognition, including accreditation or comparable vetting.

[¶126] Other formal recognition: The intermediary obtains formal regulatory recognition through a mechanism not captured by designation/notification or vetting/accreditation.

[¶127] None: The regulatory instrument does not establish a formal recognition pathway for the intermediary.

[¶128] Unclear: The available regulatory instrument does not permit reliable classification.

[¶129] Unclear/cross-reference: The recognition pathway cannot be reliably classified from the instrument being coded because its determination depends on a cross-reference to another legal source.

[¶130] N/A: No regulatory intermediary is identified, or the variable does not apply.

#### [¶131] Obligation intensity

[¶132] Definition: Identifies the intensity of the regulatory obligations imposed on the intermediary itself. The variable captures the extent to which the regulatory framework moves from basic transparency and communication requirements toward procedural controls and, ultimately, substantive requirements governing the intermediary's competence, independence, oversight, and accountability.

[¶133] Values:

[¶134] Light: Mainly contact, notification, reporting, or transparency duties.

[¶135] Moderate: Light obligations plus procedural, recordkeeping, complaint-handling, or cooperation duties.

[¶136] Strong: Moderate obligations plus requirements concerning competence, independence, ongoing surveillance, corrective action, accountability, or consequences affecting participation in the relevant market or regulatory regime.

[¶137] None: No substantive regulatory obligations are imposed on the intermediary.

[¶138] Unclear: The available regulatory instrument does not permit reliable classification.

[¶139] Unclear/cross-reference: Obligation intensity cannot be reliably classified from the instrument being coded because relevant obligations depend on a cross-reference to another legal source.

[¶140] N/A: No regulatory intermediary is identified, or the variable does not apply.

[¶141] Coding rule: Code the overall intensity of the obligations imposed on the intermediary rather than the number of individual obligations. The categories are cumulative in intensity: Moderate incorporates the types of requirements associated with Light, while Strong incorporates the types associated with Moderate and adds requirements affecting competence, independence, surveillance, corrective action, accountability, or market participation.

#### [¶142] Supervision

[¶143] Definition: Identifies and describes how the regulatory intermediary is supervised under the regulatory instrument, including the public authority responsible for oversight and the mechanisms through which the intermediary's continued compliance with the conditions governing its regulatory role is monitored.

[¶171] The codebook is conceptually quite strong, but it does not yet accurately represent the Excel dataset as it currently exists. My reading is that it describes the dataset you are trying to build, rather than documenting faithfully the dataset that is presently in the workbook.

[¶172] One problem is the unit of observation. The codebook says that instruments are first coded and then “each regulatory intermediary identified within an instrument” becomes a separate observation, so that a law with several intermediaries should appear in several rows. Yet it also defines the “unit of analysis” as the EU legislative instrument or policy document. Those two statements need to be reconciled.

[¶173] 1. What the codebook is trying to do

[¶174] There is a good underlying architecture. It distinguishes three analytical purposes:

[¶175 TABLE]
| Layer | Question | Main variables |
|---|---|---|
| Historical/institutional mapping | Where and when does intermediation emerge? | year, domain, scope, sector, instrument type |
| Governance architecture | Who governs whom, and how? | who regulates, self-regulation, intermediary, role |
| Institutional variation among intermediaries | What kind of intermediary is this and how is it governed? | functions, organizational basis, recognition, obligations, supervision |

[¶176] That three-part architecture is explicit in the codebook and makes theoretical sense.

[¶177] The third part is particularly useful because it goes beyond merely counting intermediaries and tries to characterize how regulatory intermediation is institutionally constructed: function, organizational basis, recognition pathway, obligation intensity and supervision.  So I would retain this overall architecture.

[¶178] 2. But the actual Excel file is different

[¶179] I inspected the workbook itself. It contains six sheets, not simply one homogeneous dataset:

[¶180 TABLE]
| Sheet | Approximate role |
|---|---|
| Overview of EU legislation | Selected legislative inventory |
| Fuller list of EU legislations | broader universe/inventory |
| Coding Digital Intermediaries | main instrument-level coding sheet |
| List of intermediaries | small intermediary-level pilot |
| List of actors | more developed intermediary-level restructuring |
| List of unused | excluded/unused instruments |

[¶181] The codebook presently documents the .xlsx file generically rather than explaining this internal architecture. It says merely that the Excel file contains the coding of legislative instruments and intermediaries.

[¶182] That is inadequate because these sheets represent different units and stages of the database construction.

[¶183] The principal coding sheet

[¶184] Coding Digital Intermediaries contains:   113 data rows and 31 variables.

[¶185] But those rows are overwhelmingly legislative-instrument observations, not law–intermediary observations.  Of the 113 rows:

[¶186] 41 currently contain a substantive intermediary description;

[¶187] 44 explicitly say N/A;

[¶188] 28 have no intermediary coding yet.

[¶189] More importantly, many of those 41 rows contain several intermediaries in the same cell. Examples include formulations such as:

[¶190] “Competent bodies; notified bodies; insurance companies”

[¶191] Or  “Notified bodies and testing laboratories / Insurance companies.”

[¶192] By a simple conservative check, at least 23 of the 41 substantive intermediary rows contain indications of multiple actors or arrangements.

[¶193] That directly contradicts the codebook's statement that one intermediary should occupy one observation.

[¶194] This matters enormously because the same row then produces entries such as:

[¶195] CABs: No; NABs and EA: Yes

[¶196] inside variables that the codebook says should contain only Yes / No / N/A.

[¶197] The functional coding scheme itself is clearly intended to be binary/multi-label. The present spreadsheet prevents that design from working properly because several actors are aggregated in a row.

[¶198] 3. The most important improvement: separate the two levels

[¶199] I think the cleanest solution is not one dataset but two linked tables.

[¶200] Table A — Instruments

[¶201] One row = one EU regulatory instrument.

[¶202] This is where you should put:

[¶203 TABLE]
| Instrument-level variable |
|---|
| instrument_id |
| official title |
| year |
| governance domain |
| horizontal/vertical |
| sector |
| instrument type |
| legal reference |
| legal status |
| source URL |
| who regulates |
| regulatory strategy |
| how self-regulation is operationalized |
| whether any intermediary exists |
| number of intermediary observations |

[¶204] This table is crucial because it preserves your zero cases: laws in which there is no regulatory intermediary. That is indispensable if you eventually want to study questions such as:

[¶205] Has reliance on regulatory intermediaries increased over time?

[¶206] Which sectors use intermediaries more extensively?

[¶207] Which regulatory architectures generate intermediation?

[¶208] A pure intermediary dataset cannot answer these questions properly because laws with zero intermediaries disappear from the denominator.

[¶209] Table B — Law × Intermediary

[¶210] One row = one intermediary in one regulatory instrument.

[¶211] I would make this the primary analytical observation for studying intermediation:

[¶212] Observationij=Instrumenti×IntermediaryjObservation_{ij}=Instrument_i \times Intermediary_j

[¶213] For example:

[¶214 TABLE]
| instrument_id | intermediary_id | intermediary |
|---|---|---|
| EMC-1989 | EMC-1989-01 | Competent body |
| EMC-1989 | EMC-1989-02 | Notified body |
| EMC-1989 | EMC-1989-03 | Insurance company |

[¶215] Then each can have its own:

[¶216] Assurance = Yes/No

[¶217] Monitoring = Yes/No

[¶218] Auditing = Yes/No

[¶219] Recognition pathway

[¶220] Obligation intensity

[¶221] etc.

[¶222] This single restructuring would solve a very large share of the problems I found.

[¶223] 4. Several codebook values do not match the actual data

[¶224] There are substantial discrepancies.

[¶225 TABLE]
| Variable | Codebook | Actual workbook |
|---|---|---|
| Governance domain | Information / Infrastructure / Both | Infrastructure / Information / Information and infrastructure / Unspecified |
| Instrument type | Directive / Regulation / Decision / Recommendation / Resolution | also Communication, Framework Decision, Commission Directive, Commission Regulation, Council Resolution, etc. |

| Supervision | promised structured variable | largely free-text “How are intermediaries being regulated?” |

[¶226] So the codebook should not simply be completed around the existing categories. The dataset itself first needs controlled vocabularies.

[¶227] 5. The organizational-basis variable needs conceptual redesign

[¶228] This is an especially important theoretical issue.  The codebook proposes:

[¶229] market/private-capable;

[¶230] epistemic/professional;

[¶231] associational;

[¶232] public/quasi-public.

[¶233] These are not actually mutually exclusive dimensions.  A conformity-assessment company can simultaneously be:  private + for-profit + professional/epistemic.

[¶234] A professional association can be:

[¶235] private + non-profit + associational + epistemic.

[¶236] I would therefore replace one organizational_basis variable with several dimensions:

[¶237 TABLE]
| Dimension | Examples |
|---|---|
| Ownership/control | public / private / hybrid |
| Organizational form | firm / association / professional body / NGO / public body / network |
| Profit status | for-profit / non-profit / N/A |
| Expertise basis | professional/technical expertise yes/no |
| Relationship to target | internal / external |
| Collective basis | membership association yes/no |

[¶238] This will produce a much cleaner comparative dataset.

[¶239] 6. Recognition pathways should also be multi-dimensional

[¶240] The current codebook treats recognition as roughly categorical.  But the data themselves show why this is problematic. An actor may be:

[¶241] accredited → vetted → designated → notified.

[¶242] Those are stages rather than mutually exclusive alternatives.

[¶243] I would code separate indicators such as:

[¶244] recognition_ex_lege

[¶245] recognition_accreditation

[¶246] recognition_vetting

[¶247] recognition_designation

[¶248] recognition_notification

[¶249] recognition_certification

[¶250] recognition_market_selection

[¶251] recognition_beneficiary_selection

[¶252] and then:

[¶253] recognizer_type

[¶254] This would capture something theoretically interesting: how the state creates, authorizes and legitimates intermediaries.

[¶255] 7. Obligation intensity needs to be derived rather than judged globally

[¶256] The Light–Moderate–Strong idea is good. The codebook currently defines it cumulatively.

[¶257] But this produces coder discretion. The actual data already contain:

[¶258] “Low”

[¶259] “Minimal”

[¶260] “Minimal (truly)”

[¶261] “moderate?”

[¶262] “Strong”

[¶263] “according to another Directive”

[¶264] That suggests coders are finding the global judgment difficult.

[¶265] A stronger approach would first code the underlying obligations:

[¶266 TABLE]
| Obligation dimension |
|---|
| registration/notification |
| transparency |
| reporting |
| recordkeeping |
| procedural requirements |
| competence requirements |
| independence requirements |
| conflict-of-interest rules |
| audit/surveillance |
| cooperation with regulator |
| corrective-action duties |
| liability |
| sanctions |
| withdrawal of designation/accreditation |

[¶267] You can then derive Light / Moderate / Strong according to a transparent algorithm.

[¶268] This would make the variable far more reproducible.

[¶269] 8. You need a variable distinguishing actors from mechanisms

[¶270] This became very apparent from the actual Excel file.  Your “intermediaries” currently include things such as:

[¶271] mediators;

[¶272] associations;

[¶273] conformity-assessment bodies;

[¶274] auditors;

[¶275] but also:

[¶276] internal complaint-handling systems;

[¶277] stakeholder dialogue;

[¶278] registries;

[¶279] ADR procedures;

[¶280] content moderation arrangements.

[¶281] These are ontologically different things.  Interestingly, the auxiliary List of actors sheet already begins solving this with an Observation_type variable such as:

[¶282] named_intermediary  versus  process_based_arrangement.

[¶283] I think this is extremely valuable and should move into the official codebook.

[¶284] I would call it:  intermediary_form

[¶285] with categories such as: actor / organization / organizational unit / process-based arrangement / infrastructure or platform / network.

[¶286] Otherwise, the dataset risks conflating regulatory intermediaries with mechanisms of regulatory intermediation.

[¶287] That distinction could itself become theoretically important.

[¶288] 9. Several excellent variables in the Excel file are missing from the codebook

[¶289] The List of actors sheet contains potentially important fields that the current codebook does not properly document, including:

[¶290] regulatee type, beneficiary type, regulatory strategy, position in governance chain, recognizer type, primary function/mechanism, and intermediation family.

[¶291] I would not discard these lightly.   In particular, Position_in_governance_chain could be theoretically very important because regulatory intermediation is fundamentally relational:

[¶292] Regulator→Intermediary→TargetRegulator \rightarrow Intermediary \rightarrow Target

[¶293] is very different from

[¶294] Target→Intermediary→BeneficiaryTarget \rightarrow Intermediary \rightarrow Beneficiary

[¶295] Or Intermediary↔IntermediaryIntermediary \leftrightarrow Intermediary

[¶296] or an intermediary operating inside the regulated organization.

[¶297] This may ultimately become one of the dataset's most distinctive contributions.

[¶298] 10. The function taxonomy needs sharper decision rules

[¶299] The current function list is sensible, but several categories overlap:

[¶300] assurance vs auditing

[¶301] monitoring vs auditing

[¶302] complaint handling vs dispute redress

[¶303] internal compliance vs monitoring

[¶304] enforcement vs dispute redress

[¶305] You need explicit positive and negative examples for each. For example:

[¶306] Monitoring: continuous or repeated observation/information collection without necessarily issuing an attestation.

[¶307] Auditing: structured episodic examination against specified criteria.

[¶308] Assurance: provision of an authoritative judgment, certification, verification or attestation regarding conformity.

[¶309] An auditor might therefore perform both auditing and assurance, but monitoring should not automatically imply auditing.

[¶310] The codebook presently gives definitions, but not enough boundary rules for high intercoder reliability.

[¶311] 11. The biggest documentation gaps

[¶312] There are several things that must be completed before publication. The codebook itself currently leaves the quality-assurance procedure blank and the number of cases and variables blank.  I would add a proper methodological section covering coder training, double coding, adjudication, reliability testing, coding dates, source provenance, data freeze date and version history.

[¶313] For this project in particular, I would calculate reliability separately for:

[¶314] intermediary identification, functions, actor type, recognition mechanism, and obligation intensity.

[¶315] For the binary function variables, Cohen's κ or Krippendorff's α would work. Obligation intensity would merit an ordinal reliability measure.

[¶317] At present there is no robust identifier architecture. I would introduce:

[¶318] instrument_id

[¶319] intermediary_id

[¶320] observation_id

[¶321] For example:

[¶322] EU_2022_2065

[¶323] EU_2022_2065_INT_04

[¶324] This is essential for deduplication, updates and merging.  I would also add:

[¶325] eurlex_url

[¶326] source_version

[¶327] consolidated_text_date

[¶328] retrieval_date

[¶329] status_as_of

[¶330] and ideally a short:

[¶331] coding_evidence

[¶332] field.

[¶333] The existing Relevant provision(s) idea is exactly right in principle—the codebook explicitly requires the relevant Articles, Recitals, Annexes or Appendices. But in the main coding sheet that field is almost entirely unpopulated, while the intermediary-level pilot sheet handles it much better.

[¶337] The most important conclusion is therefore:   The codebook should be treated as a description of the intended database architecture, not yet as an accurate codebook of the current dataset.

[¶338] And I would make one substantive design decision above all others: make the database explicitly multilevel. Keep an instrument-level table containing the full universe of laws, including laws without intermediaries, and a second law × intermediary table in which every intermediary has its own observation.

[¶339] That will make the historical, regulatory-governance and intermediation analyses all compatible rather than forcing three different analytical purposes into the same row structure. It would also allow the List of actors sheet—which already looks like the beginning of the right architecture—to become the basis of the real intermediary dataset.
