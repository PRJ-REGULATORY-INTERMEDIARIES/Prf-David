[¶2] Regulatory Intermediation in Digital Governance

## [¶3] Purpose and method

[¶4 TABLE]
| This chart identifies the principal regulatory intermediaries established or recognized in European digital-governance instruments. Regulatory intermediation is interpreted through the polycentric governance and enhanced self-regulation framework developed by Medzini and Levi-Faur. The analysis focuses on regulatory intermediaries that perform regulatory functions, including developing codes of conduct, providing assurance, monitoring compliance, administering internal compliance systems, resolving disputes and complaints, conducting audits, underwriting risk, and supporting enforcement. / The dataset covers legislative instruments and policy documents, including Directives, Regulations, or Decisions, adopted between 1976 and 2026. We coded in two stages. First, we coded each regulatory instrument as the primary unit of analysis. Second, we coded each intermediary identified within an instrument as a separate observation. Accordingly, an instrument that establishes or recognizes multiple intermediaries appears in multiple rows, with one row for each intermediary. This structure enables intermediary-level analysis while preserving the characteristics of the underlying regulatory instrument. / Each instrument is classified according to: / whether it concerns information governance, infrastructure governance, or both; / whether its regulatory scope is horizontal or sector-specific; / the sector it covers; / its instrument type, such as a Directive, Regulation, Decision, Recommendation, or Resolution; / its formal reference code and year of enactment; and / its current legal status, where applicable. / The analysis then addresses three principal questions. / First, who regulates? This question captures how regulatory authority is distributed among European Union institutions, Member States, specifically designated national authorities, and other regulatory actors. It distinguishes general references to Member States from references to named authorities, such as competent authorities, national regulatory authorities, supervisory authorities, or the European Commission, thereby capturing the degree of institutional specificity with which regulatory authority is allocated. / Second, how is self-regulation operationalized? This question assesses and describes how each instrument combines prescriptive legal requirements with process-based, performance-based, principle-based, or other forms of regulation that rely on regulated entities or third parties to implement regulatory objectives. / Third, who are the regulatory intermediaries, what functions do they perform, and how are they themselves regulated? Each intermediary is identified and coded separately. A dedicated field records the specific legal basis for its role: Relevant provision(s) (Article / Recital / Annex / Appendix). Where several provisions define the same intermediary’s role, we list all relevant provisions. / Each intermediary is coded according to the regulatory functions it performs. The function variables are code creation, assurance, monitoring, internal compliance, dispute resolution, complaint handling, auditing, insurance or risk underwriting, and enforcement. Each function is coded Yes, No, or N/A, depending on whether the function is assigned to the intermediary, expressly absent, or not applicable. / Intermediaries are also classified by actor type, including for-profit actors, industry associations, societal or epistemic actors, public bodies, public–private hybrids, unspecified, and N/A. / Additional coding records the intermediary’s recognition pathway: the legal or institutional mechanism through which it is established, selected, designated, approved, accredited, notified, vetted, or otherwise recognized. / Finally, obligation intensity captures the strength of the duties imposed on the intermediary: / Light: primarily contact, notification, reporting, or transparency duties. / Moderate: light obligations plus procedural, recordkeeping, complaint-handling, or cooperation duties. / Strong: moderate obligations plus requirements concerning competence, independence, ongoing surveillance, corrective action, accountability, or consequences affecting market access or participation. |
